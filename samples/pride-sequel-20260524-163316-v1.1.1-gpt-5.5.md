# The Ruin of Mrs. Wickham

## Synopsis

The Ruin of Mrs. Wickham

### Chapter 1: Mrs. Wickham in Newcastle

- Two years after her marriage, Lydia Wickham is found in Newcastle, reduced from the triumph of being “the first married” to the less dazzling distinction of being least remembered with pleasure by her relations.
- Mr. Wickham, still possessed of a handsome countenance and an excellent opinion of his own injuries, has converted disappointment into habitual idleness, and idleness into such little expedients as are seldom named in respectable rooms.
- Lydia endeavours to preserve the air of a woman much admired, though her gowns are contrived, her ribbons revived beyond their natural term, and her jewels appear and disappear with a regularity not wholly owing to fashion.
- Her society declines by degrees; she is received among persons whose gaiety is more noisy than creditable, and whose compliments, though abundant, are of a sort which a wiser woman would not have collected.
- Certain cordial draughts and “tonics” become familiar in the Wickham household, recommended by no physician of delicacy, yet valued for lending courage where principle had never been strong.

### Chapter 2: The Price of Being Neglected

- Lydia writes alternately to Jane and Elizabeth, her requests disguised as affection, her complaints as sprightliness, and her resentments as little jokes which deceive nobody but herself.
- Jane Bingley answers with kindness and such assistance as prudence permits; Elizabeth Darcy answers less often, but with a steadiness which Lydia interprets as pride, because it is always convenient to give virtue an ill name.
- Wickham’s debts, once merely inconvenient, become pressing; his companions in Newcastle are gentlemen of dubious vigour and repute, who admire his address while valuing him chiefly for what mischief it may perform.
- Lydia, finding admiration cheaper than respect, begins to frequent circles where a lady may be praised very lavishly on condition that she forgets to inquire too closely into the meaning of the praise.
- The remembrance of Pemberley, first a source of envy and then of calculation, returns to both husband and wife; Lydia recalls cupboards, jewels, servants’ habits, and the easy confidence with which Elizabeth had once suffered a sister to wander through her happiness.

### Chapter 3: A Visit Not Paid

- News reaches the Wickhams that Mr. and Mrs. Darcy are to be absent from Pemberley Cottage, a smaller residence upon the estate, where many intimate articles and some family pieces remain in careless security.
- Wickham, with that ingenuity which might have made him useful had it ever submitted to honesty, persuades Lydia that the world has long been in their debt, and that only the dull call restitution by another name.
- Disguised with sufficient plainness to be unremarkable, and assisted by their knowledge of the estate and its servants’ routines, they contrive a bold entrance in open day, when suspicion is least inclined to be awake.
- Lydia moves through the rooms with a strange mixture of triumph and irritation, laying hands upon silver, jewels, and heirlooms, while telling herself that Elizabeth has more than any one woman can require.
- The theft succeeds with an ease almost indecorous; objects of family affection and ancient consequence are removed in parcels too ordinary to alarm the indifferent, their value approaching the astonishing sum of one hundred thousand pounds.

### Chapter 4: London Reckonings

- The Wickhams arrive in London, where conscience is not unknown, but is often obliged to take very inconvenient lodgings; there they seek acquaintances capable of turning ancestral honour into ready money.
- The heirlooms of Pemberley pass into obscurer hands, diminished at each exchange by greed, fear, and vulgar bargaining, while Wickham comforts himself that only fools insist upon the full worth of stolen dignity.
- Lydia is at first exhilarated by their apparent fortune: new gowns are ordered, better rooms secured, and every mirror consulted as if it might restore the girl who had run away to Brighton.
- Yet the success begins to trouble her in small, unromantic ways; she dreams of Elizabeth’s calm eyes, Jane’s sorrowful kindness, and Mr. Darcy’s silence, which she had always found more oppressive than reproach.
- Wickham, fearing discovery and already weary of caution, determines that England has become ungrateful and unsafe; France is proposed as a country where a gentleman of talents may begin again, especially when beginning again is paid for by another man’s plate.

### Chapter 5: The Channel

- The Wickhams board a vessel bound for France, carrying what remains of their transformed fortune, their mutual distrust, and that species of hope which is only another name for being not yet found out.
- During the crossing, rough weather overtakes them; the confusion is great, though not so great as the confusion which had long governed their conduct, and the sea proves less susceptible than society to charm or importunity.
- Wickham disappears in the disorder with an abruptness wholly unbecoming a man who had always contrived to leave last words to others; Lydia is left clinging to a broken piece of timber, the shore invisible and her finery of no present use.
- In the cold interval before the end, Lydia thinks of Longbourn, of Jane’s sweetness, of Elizabeth’s guarded affection, and of the life which might have been hers had admiration not seemed dearer than esteem.
- Her reflections are not wholly purified: she still feels that Elizabeth had been fortunate beyond justice, that Jane had been loved too easily, and that she herself had never been properly understood; yet beneath these poor consolations comes a late, bare perception that she had mistaken noise for happiness and defiance for strength.
- The sea closes over her without spectacle; and if the world is not much improved by the removal of Mrs. Wickham, it is at least spared the further expense of her spirits, while Pemberley is left to mourn less for silver than for the profanation of trust.

## Full Text

## Chapter I  
### Mrs. Wickham in Newcastle

It is seldom that a young lady, whose earliest ambition has been to be married before her sisters, is prepared to discover, after the attainment of that distinction, how little remains to be done; and how very much may be suffered by a person who has gained her object without having considered what use she should make of it. Mrs. Wickham had once thought herself peculiarly favoured in being the first of Mr. Bennet’s daughters who could sign herself by another name; and if the name had been accompanied by more guineas, more equipages, and a greater number of people disposed to admire it, her triumph might have lasted longer. But two years of matrimony had done much to reduce the brilliancy of an event which, at sixteen, had appeared to her the natural conclusion of all felicity.

When Captain Wickham’s regiment was removed to Newcastle, Mrs. Wickham accompanied him with a spirits proper to her age, and a wardrobe very improper to her circumstances. She had quitted Longbourn in disgrace, entered upon marriage with congratulation chiefly from herself, and been received in every new quarter with a degree of notice which she mistook for consequence. To be the wife of a handsome officer was still something; to be the wife of Mr. Wickham was, in her own estimation, a great deal more. She had not yet learned that a handsome countenance, like a fashionable bonnet, may be worn very often, and yet be of small service when the weather changes.

Newcastle, at first, pleased her exceedingly. There were assemblies, walks, officers’ wives, shop-windows, and people who did not know all that had passed in Hertfordshire. This last circumstance was so much in its favour, that Lydia declared she should like the place above all others; and for some weeks she did like it, as she liked every thing that promised amusement and asked no reflection. She laughed, danced, talked, and was admired by such persons as are always willing to admire youth, good-humour, and forwardness, when they are not obliged to maintain them.

But admiration, though Lydia had never suspected it, is one of those commodities which must be supported by some income, either of fortune, beauty, or novelty. Fortune she had none; beauty, though still considerable, had been a little injured by late hours, disappointment, and the habit of thinking rouge an economy; and novelty, in a garrison town, is consumed with a rapidity which no prudence can long withstand. By the time Mrs. Wickham had told every story of her elopement which could be made agreeable to herself, and had hinted, with all the elegance of which she was mistress, at the greatness of her connexions, people began to remember that Mrs. Darcy had never visited her, that Mrs. Bingley’s letters contained more affection than bank-notes, and that Mr. Wickham’s debts were spoken of by tradesmen with a freedom not usual towards the prosperous.

Mr. Wickham bore these untoward circumstances with all the grace of a man who had long practised being injured. He was still handsome; he could still bow, smile, and address a lady with that mixture of deference and interest which makes common sense unnecessary for at least ten minutes; and he still possessed, in its fullest bloom, the conviction that the world had used him very ill. This persuasion was the more convenient, as it relieved him from the fatigue of using the world very well in return. He had grievances against Mr. Darcy, against the army, against fortune, against creditors, and sometimes, when dinner was ill-dressed or delayed, against Mrs. Wickham; but as his complaints were delivered with ease, and generally ended in a jest, Lydia heard them with complacency, and thought him the most agreeable of men, except when he wanted money.

The want of money, however, has this unfortunate quality, that it will not remain a subject for conversation only. It presents itself at breakfast in the shape of an unpaid baker; at dinner under the more substantial form of a butcher; and at night, with peculiar want of delicacy, in the remembrance that candles, coals, lodging, dress, and pleasure are all governed by the same vulgar necessity. Lydia had been brought up in a house where economy was often recommended and seldom practised; and she had early learned to consider a bill as a thing which might be troublesome, but could not be serious, if only one did not look at it long. Mr. Wickham’s principles were, in this respect, entirely congenial. He thought a creditor impertinent if he pressed, unreasonable if he waited, and ungenerous if he expected payment at all.

Their lodgings, which had at first been taken in a street sufficiently genteel to be named in letters, were gradually exchanged for apartments of less pretension and more convenience to concealment. Lydia called the change “vastly snug,” and observed that she was quite tired of being always in view of stupid people; but she did not add, what she could hardly have failed to perceive, that the staircase was narrow, the paper stained, and the parlour chimney smoked whenever the wind was in a humour to tell the truth. A little drawing-room, whose chief ornament was a cracked mirror in a gilt frame, became the theatre of her efforts to remain charming; and it must be owned that she performed the part with perseverance. Her gowns, which had once fluttered with reckless freshness, were now contrived with much ingenuity; a faded muslin was made new by a bow; a soiled ribbon was revived by candle-light; a shawl, which had seen better days, was taught by folding to conceal the greater part of its history. Lydia had always possessed a talent for believing that whatever she wore must become fashionable, because she wore it with confidence; and confidence, in a young woman, is sometimes accepted for elegance by those who have no particular interest in distinguishing them.

Her jewels were of a more uncertain nature. Some appeared suddenly, with explanations too lively to be exact; others vanished with equal rapidity, under pretences of alteration, cleaning, or lending to a friend. A little locket from her mother, a pair of earrings once bestowed by Mr. Wickham in the first splendour of their attachment and the last stage of his credit, a brooch which Jane had sent with more kindness than judgment,—all, at different periods, were absent from her person. Lydia never said they were pawned. Such a word would have been exceedingly disagreeable, and she had a natural aversion to every expression which made a circumstance look poorer than was absolutely necessary. They were “gone to be mended,” “left with a woman who understood such things,” or “put by for a particular occasion.” It was one advantage of her temper that, if she deceived nobody else, she very often deceived herself.

Her letters home, at this period, preserved much of their former sprightliness. She had always been a correspondent who preferred exclamation to information; and though her situation had altered, her manner had not. She wrote to her mother of officers, feathers, parties, and the shocking price of lace; to Jane of her dear little wants, introduced as trifles which she was almost ashamed to mention; and to Elizabeth very seldom, because Elizabeth’s answers had a provoking habit of being reasonable. Mrs. Bennet, who still considered Lydia’s marriage a triumph dimmed only by the want of a better settlement, lamented, praised, wondered, and could do nothing. Jane, from her own income and Mr. Bingley’s indulgence, sometimes assisted; but Jane’s assistance had the fault of being accompanied by gentle hopes that Lydia would be prudent. Lydia preferred a present without a moral, and was apt to tell Wickham, upon receiving such letters, that dear Jane was grown quite grave since her marriage, though still the best creature in the world.

Of Elizabeth she spoke with less tenderness. The contrast between Pemberley and Mrs. Wickham’s lodgings was one which even Lydia could see; and as she had never been fond of any comparison in which she was not herself the ornament, she very naturally concluded that her sister’s prosperity must be pride. Elizabeth’s reserve, which had once been attributed to envy, was now imputed to importance. “Lizzy was always high,” Lydia would say, tossing her head; “and now she has got such a great house, and such a husband, there is no bearing her. I dare say she thinks nobody can be genteel without ten servants and a wilderness of woods. But I was married before her, after all.”

This last reflection had been her consolation so often that it had lost nothing of its value by repetition. There are minds to which chronology is morality; and Lydia, having once preceded her elder sisters to the altar, could never entirely believe herself behind them in happiness.

The society into which the Wickhams were first admitted in Newcastle contained many persons who were not scrupulous, but who were still desirous of appearing so. This was at first sufficient restraint upon Lydia’s imprudence, and sufficient encouragement to her vanity. She was invited, laughed with, rallied, and sometimes envied by young women whose fortunes were little better than her own, and whose husbands had not half Mr. Wickham’s address. But as months passed, and dinners were not returned, subscriptions not paid, and small obligations not remembered unless they were owing to Mrs. Wickham, invitations diminished. The more respectable ladies discovered that she was “very lively,” which is often the first phrase by which society prepares itself to be less intimate; and the officers’ wives, who had been delighted with her at a distance, began to feel that her spirits, when too near, were an expensive entertainment.

Lydia did not at once perceive the change; or, perceiving it, gave it the name most agreeable to herself. She was tired of stiff people; she hated formal parties; she liked those who were merry and said what they meant. This preference led her, by degrees, into company where merriment was certainly abundant, and meaning perhaps more intelligible than delicate. There were houses in the town where music was louder than conversation, where cards were played with a freedom more animated than exact, and where gentlemen who had no appointments and ladies whose appellations varied with the person who introduced them, assembled with a cordiality which sheer respectability could seldom equal. In such circles Mrs. Wickham was received with warmth. She was pretty, she was bold, she was an officer’s wife, and she laughed at every thing; qualifications which, in certain rooms, are nearly equivalent to birth, fortune, and character.

It would be unjust to say that Lydia understood at once the nature of all the admiration bestowed upon her. She had been accustomed from girlhood to collect compliments as children collect shells, without much regard to their value or cleanliness; and if some of those now offered were of a sort which a wiser woman would have left upon the shore, Lydia saw only that they were numerous. She liked to be told she was charming, though the speaker’s coat was shabby and his eyes too familiar; she liked to be begged for a song, though the room had grown noisy and the hour late; she liked to fancy herself the object of envy among women whose envy was the least respectable thing they possessed. There were moments when a look, a whisper, or a laugh, made even her colour rise; but she dismissed the sensation as prudery, a weakness she had always despised in others and was determined never to encourage in herself.

Mr. Wickham, whose discernment in matters of danger was quick whenever danger did not concern another’s peace, saw more than Lydia, and was not displeased. He was not a man to object to his wife’s being admired, provided the admiration brought him convenience, credit, or amusement. Indeed he sometimes praised her conquests with a jocularity that would have wounded a woman of delicacy and frightened one of principle. Lydia, having neither delicacy enough to be wounded nor principle enough to be frightened, was only flattered. If her husband was sometimes absent for whole evenings, if he returned with spirits too high or too low for explanation, if he spoke of gentlemen whose names she could never quite remember and whose professions seemed to consist chiefly in knowing where money might be found, she asked few questions. She had no taste for inquiry unless the answer promised diversion.

Mr. Wickham’s pursuits, meanwhile, had taken a direction which he would have called resource, though a harsher judge might have found some other term for it. His pay was irregularly anticipated, his credit every where exhausted, and his patience with honest poverty—never great—entirely worn out. He played when he could, borrowed when he might, and when neither play nor borrowing answered, found little offices for his ingenuity which did not exactly fall within the instructions of his Colonel. A horse changed owners under circumstances of singular confusion; a watch, lost by one gentleman after supper, was recovered by another before breakfast; notes of hand passed through several pockets, acquiring at each stage a more doubtful complexion. Wickham was never detected in any thing. Detection, indeed, is often the only circumstance which converts fashionable irregularity into crime; and he had still enough of address, beauty, and hardihood to keep misfortune in the genteeler class of misunderstanding.

He met, too, with companions well fitted to encourage his genius. They were men who talked much of honour, because they had no other security to offer; who praised spirit in proportion as it approached swindling; and who considered the laws of property a useful arrangement for those who had property to lose. Such gentlemen admired Wickham sincerely. His manners gave elegance to their schemes; his connexions gave them hope; his wrongs, as he related them, gave a kind of magnanimity to every project by which another man’s purse might be lightened. To hear him speak of Mr. Darcy was to suppose that Providence had committed a grave oversight, and that the estate of Pemberley ought, in common decency, to have been placed at his disposal.

It must not be imagined that Mrs. Wickham’s household was without its little comforts. There are consolations suited to every station; and where prudence is absent, some substitute is generally found. Among the Wickhams, certain cordial draughts began to be valued for their power of reconciling the mind to circumstances which, in a soberer hour, might have appeared too plain. Lydia had first taken them for a cold, then for lowness, then because Mrs. Younge—an acquaintance of animated manners and uncertain residence—declared that no woman could keep her looks in a northern climate without assistance. The assistance was of that kind not usually recommended by physicians of delicacy; but it had immediate effects, and Lydia was never fond of benefits which required waiting for. It brightened her eyes, warmed her spirits, and made the shabby parlour, for half an hour, almost as good as a ball-room.

Mr. Wickham had his own restoratives, taken in company with gentlemen of dubious vigour and repute, whose friendship was cemented less by esteem than by the impossibility of being severe upon one another. If he sometimes returned with a flushed cheek and a temper alternately caressing and savage, Lydia thought it only the way of men who had affairs on their mind. If she herself, after a small glass recommended as a tonic, became louder, kinder, more resentful, or more disposed to weep over her own ill-usage, she attributed it to sensibility. There is no failing which vanity cannot dignify, when it has sufficient practice.

One evening, near the close of a very wet November day, when the fog had folded the street in a dirty obscurity and the fire in Mrs. Wickham’s parlour had sunk to that state in which it gives more reproach than heat, Lydia sat before the cracked mirror endeavouring to restore a curl. Her gown had been turned, trimmed, and altered till it might almost have claimed the merit of resignation; and a necklace of coloured stones, which owed more to candle-light than to nature, lay upon the table beside her. She had been invited, or believed herself invited, to a supper at Mrs. Ford’s—one of those ladies whose acquaintance is easily made by people already going down in the world, and with difficulty acknowledged by those who have any intention of rising.

“Wickham,” cried she, after surveying herself with more hope than satisfaction, “do look at this ribbon. Is it not the most shocking thing you ever saw? I declare it has no more colour than Lizzy Darcy when one tells her something she does not like.”

Mr. Wickham, stretched in a chair with the elegant negligence of a man who has nothing to do and does it perfectly, raised his eyes from a newspaper three days old.

“It has seen service,” said he. “Like its mistress, it deserves a better fortune.”

Lydia laughed, for a compliment was always a compliment, though wrapped in misfortune. “La! how you talk. I am sure I should have better fortune if my sisters behaved handsomely. Only think of Lizzy, with all that silver and all those jewels, and me obliged to wear this odious thing. Jane sent me five pounds last month, to be sure; dear Jane!—but five pounds does not go far when one has any notion of dressing.”

“Mrs. Darcy,” replied Wickham, folding the paper with care, “has acquired the habit, common to persons suddenly elevated, of considering money a proof of virtue when kept, and a proof of folly when asked for.”

“Exactly so! I always said Lizzy was proud. She may call it prudence, but I know what prudence means in a rich woman. It means keeping every thing to herself.”

Wickham smiled; not the open smile with which he had once deceived half Meryton, but a slighter one, formed rather by habit than pleasure. “Pemberley is a noble place.”

“I dare say,” cried Lydia, turning again to the glass. “Though I was not let to enjoy it half so much as I ought. Lizzy was always watching me, as if I was going to run away with the chimney-pieces. Such airs!—and Mr. Darcy looking as grave as a judge, only because one laughs a little. I am sure I never saw a house where people seemed so afraid of being amused.”

“Some houses,” said Wickham, “are dull from pride; others from conscience. Pemberley has perhaps a sufficiency of both.”

Lydia was pleased with this, though she did not perfectly understand it. She put the ribbon to her hair, rejected it, tried it at her waist, rejected it again, and at last declared it vastly improved by doing nothing at all.

A knock at the door interrupted her. It was not the knock of a friend, nor yet exactly that of a servant; it had the timid resolution of an unpaid account. Their maid-of-all-work, a girl whose honesty had been preserved chiefly by lack of opportunity, admitted a small tradesman’s boy with a parcel and a message. Mrs. Wickham seized the parcel with delight, for it contained a pair of gloves; but the message, being an inquiry after a former balance, was less agreeable.

“Tell Mr. Harker,” said Lydia, with dignity, “that Captain Wickham will call himself.”

The boy looked doubtful. Mr. Wickham looked charming. “Certainly,” he said. “To-morrow, perhaps; or the next day. Mr. Harker is a worthy fellow, though occasionally impatient.”

The boy departed, unconvinced; and Lydia, forgetting the debt in the gloves, tried them on immediately. They were a little tight, but she declared that a tight glove always looked genteel.

When she had finished admiring her hands, she found Mr. Wickham watching her with an expression which, had she been more observant, might have suggested calculation rather than fondness.

“You are resolved on Mrs. Ford’s to-night?” said he.

“To be sure I am. Captain Morris promised to sing; and there is a new gentleman from Shields who says I am the finest dancer in the North. Besides, I am moped to death here.”

“You are much admired there.”

“I know I am,” said Lydia simply.

“And admiration is sometimes useful.”

She looked at him then, half laughing, half puzzled. “Useful! Lord, Wickham, how grave you are. I like admiration because it is pleasant.”

“Pleasure and use are not always enemies.” He rose, came behind her chair, and, resting one hand upon it, surveyed both their reflections in the glass. They made, perhaps, a handsome picture still: the man graceful and insinuating, the woman blooming enough to be mistaken for happy by those who did not know the expense of her bloom. “You were made for better things, Lydia.”

“So I was,” cried she, instantly animated. “I always said so. If papa had done as he ought, or if my uncle had settled something handsome, or if Lizzy—”

“Yes,” said Wickham softly, “if Elizabeth had been less determined to keep every advantage.”

“That is just what I mean. She has every thing. Jane too—though Jane is so good one cannot be angry with her long. But Lizzy! To think of her mistress of Pemberley, and me—” She stopped, for even Lydia could not always make a jest out of the room she sat in.

Mr. Wickham, who had the art of allowing silence to perform the labour of persuasion, said nothing for a moment. Then, with a change of tone, he observed that the evening would be late, that she had better take her cloak, and that if Mrs. Ford’s party proved amusing, she need not hurry home.

This permission, given with the careless ease of a liberal husband, delighted Lydia. That it might have contained any thing less flattering than confidence never occurred to her. She kissed him, called him a dear creature, complained again of her ribbon, drank a little of the cordial which Mrs. Younge had recommended, and went out glowing with artificial spirits into the damp and smoke of Newcastle.

Mr. Wickham remained some minutes after her departure without moving. The fire had sunk lower; the room looked meaner when Lydia’s animation no longer disputed the fact. At length he took from his pocket two letters, one unopened, the other read and folded to softness. The unopened was from a creditor; the other from a companion whose address was seldom written twice in the same hand. He considered them both, threw the first upon the table, and put the second carefully away.

There are men who, when pressed by necessity, are awakened to exertion; and there are others in whom necessity only sharpens the faculty of blaming their friends. Mr. Wickham belonged to both classes in a manner peculiarly his own. He could exert himself when the exertion promised profit without labour, and he could blame his friends with a spirit which no disappointment ever impaired. As he sat in that poor parlour, with the rain beating against the window and the remains of Lydia’s toilette scattered round him, his thoughts travelled, not to the tradesmen he owed, nor to the wife he neglected, nor to the follies which had brought him there, but to Derbyshire, to woods, waters, plate, pictures, and possessions which had once seemed almost within his reach because he had wished for them very much.

It is a common error among the vain to suppose that desire establishes a claim. Mr. Wickham had improved the doctrine by adding that disappointment creates a debt.

Lydia returned late, noisy, triumphant, and very cold. She had danced, she had been praised, she had lost more at cards than she remembered having staked, and she had been treated with particular attention by the gentleman from Shields, whose admiration was of a warmth not entirely regulated by respect. She related every thing, omitted what she did not understand, exaggerated what flattered her, and ended by declaring that respectable people were the dullest creatures in the world.

Mr. Wickham listened, smiled, and gave her another glass of Mrs. Younge’s restorative, because she looked fatigued. She drank it readily, laughed at nothing for several minutes, then suddenly shed a few tears because Jane was so happy and Elizabeth so proud, and at last fell asleep in her chair, with the tight gloves still upon her hands.

Her husband looked at her with an expression in which pity had no considerable share. Yet perhaps, had he been capable of tracing consequences with any feeling but resentment, he might have seen in that slumbering figure something more melancholy than ridiculous: a young woman still scarcely past girlhood, with beauty not wholly gone, spirits not naturally cruel, and a heart which, if it had ever been taught to value esteem above notice, might perhaps have been saved from mistaking degradation for triumph. But such reflections are troublesome guests, and Mr. Wickham had never encouraged their visits.

He rang for the maid, ordered more coal upon credit, and sat down once more to consider the advantages of having been ill-used by the world. Thus passed an evening in the household of the Wickhams: with vanity for entertainment, resentment for principle, and want for a companion too constant to be excluded. If Mrs. Wickham still believed herself admired, and Mr. Wickham still believed himself injured, they possessed between them two consolations which had survived almost every other possession; and though neither was very nourishing, both were, for the present, sufficient to keep reflection at a distance.

## Chapter II  
### The Price of Being Neglected

If there be a sensation more mortifying to a young woman of lively spirits than that of being blamed, it is that of being forgotten. Blame, at least, supposes consequence; it owns the offender still powerful enough to disturb the peace of her friends; but neglect is a sentence passed without the civility of a hearing. Mrs. Wickham, who could have supported a great deal of reproof, had it been accompanied by bustle, tears, and repeated mention of her name, found herself very ill prepared for the quieter discipline of being left to her own reflections; and as her reflections were neither numerous nor agreeable, she was obliged to resent the omission with all the warmth of injured merit.

Her correspondence, which had once been the terror and delight of Longbourn, now became her principal resource against obscurity. To write letters cost little, if one did not count postage, and promised much, if one possessed enough confidence in the tenderness, weakness, or culpable prosperity of one’s relations. Lydia had never been deficient in confidence. She wrote rapidly, with a profusion of underlinings, exclamations, and affectionate appellations, which made her wants look, at first sight, like overflowings of the heart. A request, when properly introduced by “my dearest Jane,” and concluded with “you cannot think how I long to see you,” had, in her opinion, so much the air of sisterly attachment that only a very cold person could perceive any thing pecuniary in it.

To Jane she wrote most frequently. Jane’s goodness had, from childhood, been considered by her younger sisters as a family convenience; and Lydia, who respected no virtue which did not at some time pay her bill, felt that Mrs. Bingley’s sweetness was one of those blessings of Providence which ought not to be unemployed.

“My dearest Jane,” she began, on a morning when the landlady had hinted, with vulgar precision, at six weeks’ arrears, “I am sure you will be vastly sorry to hear that I am grown quite thin with low spirits, though everybody says I never looked better, which is a great comfort. Newcastle is the most shocking place in the world for expenses; one cannot stir without guineas flying away, and yet there is nothing worth buying, except some ribbons I saw yesterday, the prettiest in nature, and so cheap that I should be quite wrong not to have them, only Wickham says we must wait till next week. He is the best creature alive, and so cheerful under all his troubles; but I own it hurts me to see him distressed, when a very little would set every thing to rights. Pray do not mention this to Lizzy, for she would only preach, and I cannot bear preaching from people who have every thing they wish. If you could send me twenty pounds, or even fifteen, I should think it the greatest kindness in the world, and would certainly repay you very soon, for Wickham has expectations which he cannot explain at present, only they are quite certain.”

Jane read such letters with pain, answered them with tenderness, and, when she could, enclosed assistance. Mr. Bingley, who had all the generosity of a man whose fortune and temper were equally easy, seldom opposed her; yet even his good-nature, under the influence of marriage and Mr. Darcy’s friendship, had acquired a small acquaintance with prudence. He disliked to see his wife distressed, and he disliked still more to strengthen the improvidence which distressed her. Ten pounds were therefore sometimes sent where twenty had been solicited, and advice accompanied what charity could not deny.

Jane’s replies were affectionate, guarded, and more useful than pleasing. She wrote of health, of economy, of the comfort of regular habits, of the impropriety of late hours, and of her earnest hope that Lydia would avoid acquaintances whose attentions could not add to her respectability. Lydia, on receiving such letters, kissed the bank-note, tossed the counsel aside, and declared that dear Jane was the best creature in the world, though married people always grew prodigiously dull when they had nothing to vex them.

Elizabeth was less easily worked upon, and therefore more severely condemned. There was, in Mrs. Darcy’s correspondence, a want of diffuseness which Lydia felt as an injury. Elizabeth loved her sister enough to be anxious for her, but not enough to be deceived by every turn of her pen; and Lydia, who had no objection to affection when it was blind, thought discernment a very unamiable addition to sisterly regard. Mrs. Darcy sent money rarely, and never without such clear expressions of the purpose to which it was to be applied, as made the gift resemble an obligation and deprived it of half its elegance. She more than once paid a tradesman directly; an expedient which Lydia pronounced “the proudest thing imaginable,” though the tradesman in question was less offended.

One of Elizabeth’s letters, written after a very moving epistle from Lydia upon the impossibility of appearing at any assembly for want of a gown, had the misfortune to be quite rational.

“My dear Lydia,” it said, “I am sorry that your situation continues to give you uneasiness, and most sincerely wish it were in my power to believe that money alone would remove it. You will not, I hope, think me unkind if I say that your comfort must depend less upon occasional assistance from your friends than upon some steady resolution in yourself and Wickham to live strictly within your means. Jane has written to me with concern; she is ever eager to help you, and I need not tell you how much her happiness is affected by yours. But we cannot do you any real service by encouraging expenses which must soon renew the same distress. Darcy desires me to add that if Wickham will state, with exactness, the nature of his debts and his present prospects, some arrangement may perhaps be considered; but it must be made openly, and with a promise, on his part, to avoid further engagements.”

There was a great deal in this to offend Mrs. Wickham. That Mr. Darcy should “desire to add” any thing was, in itself, sufficiently provoking; that Wickham should be required to state exact sums was an insult to a gentleman; and that Elizabeth should write as if prudence were better than a new gown was a proof of that unfeeling superiority which Lydia had always suspected in her, whenever Elizabeth did not do exactly as she wished.

“Openly!” cried she, with much indignation, throwing the letter across the breakfast table. “As if Wickham ever did any thing secretly! And debts!—what are debts? Everybody has debts. I dare say Mr. Darcy has debts, only nobody dares ask him for them, because he looks so disagreeable.”

Mr. Wickham, who had read the letter with attention enough to discern that it contained no remittance, smiled in a manner which did equal credit to his resentment and his self-command.

“Darcy’s notion of generosity,” said he, “has always been to make a man confess himself a beggar before he is permitted to be obliged. It is a species of benevolence peculiar to great estates.”

“And Lizzy writes as if she were my mother,” continued Lydia. “I am sure mamma never plagued me half so much, and she had a better right.”

“Your sister has gained much by marriage,” replied he; “among other things, the privilege of considering all who are less fortunate as less deserving.”

This observation was so agreeable to Lydia that she repeated it, with variations, to every person who would listen; and as the circle which still listened to her was not distinguished by excessive charity towards the prosperous, it was received with great approbation. Mrs. Ford declared Mrs. Darcy must be odious; Captain Morris swore that all rich husbands made their wives insolent; and a thin lady in yellow, who was known by three different names and answered readily to each, said that she had always observed your proud women were the worst-hearted creatures alive. Lydia went home in high spirits, persuaded that she had the judgement of society on her side; and society, in that instance, cost her only the price of two glasses of negus and a small loss at loo.

The Wickhams’ debts, in the mean time, advanced with a steadiness which might have been respectable had it belonged to any thing else. Creditors who had once bowed began to knock; those who had once knocked began to wait; and those who had waited began to speak in tones very unfavourable to illusion. The butcher, being a man of coarse habits, could not be made to understand that Captain Wickham’s expectations ought to feed him in the interim; the laundress, though more patient, became at length philosophical on the subject of linen; and the landlady, who had seen many pretty ladies pass through her rooms into worse ones, preserved a civility so edged with experience that Lydia hated her particularly.

It was Mr. Wickham’s distinction to be more offended by the manner of a demand than by the justice of it. He could forgive a man for owing him money, had such a case ever occurred, but that any one should ask money of him appeared an outrage upon the delicacy of intercourse. His temper, always elegant in prosperity, had of late acquired little inequalities. He was still capable of charming a stranger, soothing a woman, or persuading a fool; but at home, where none of these accomplishments produced immediate profit, he sometimes let them sleep. Lydia, who had married him in the comfortable belief that good looks were a provision against every evil, began occasionally to discover that a handsome man may be out of humour with as little beauty as an ugly one.

Yet their union, if not happy, was not without agreement. They were perfectly of one mind in thinking themselves ill-used, and very nearly of one mind as to the persons who had ill-used them. Mr. Darcy stood first; Elizabeth almost equalled him; Mr. Bennet was censured whenever memory supplied the notion of a father; and the world in general filled up the remaining deficiencies. Jane was seldom accused for more than weakness, because she still sent ten-pound notes; and Mr. Bingley, though rich, was forgiven by Lydia in consideration of his being “such a simple dear creature,” which meant that he had not yet learned to refuse his wife.

Wickham’s companions were now less connected with the regiment than with those regions of a town where idle men assemble to prove that occupation is unnecessary. There was Mr. Denny—no relation to the amiable officer once known in Meryton, though Lydia, from old associations, at first liked his name—who possessed the appearance of a gentleman by candle-light and the principles of one nowhere. There was Captain Blount, who had left more than one service under circumstances which he called political; Mr. Skevington, a person of much information respecting races, wagers, and private doors; and a grave, soft-spoken man named Rudge, who never laughed, never drank beyond calculation, and never forgot the value of any object once described to him. These gentlemen found Wickham an acquisition. He gave an air to their meetings; his manners lent fluency to proposals which, in less polished mouths, might have seemed abrupt; and his connexion with families of station furnished him with recollections which they valued as other men value introductions.

They met at rooms where the chairs were broken by conviviality rather than age, where the waiters knew when to enter and when to be blind, and where cards were played with that freedom which leaves every loser uncertain whether he has been unfortunate or instructed. Wickham, who had once had scruples enough to make vice interesting, had now almost outlived the inconvenience; yet even he, in the society of Mr. Rudge, felt occasionally that he was still a beginner. Mr. Rudge heard every thing, advised little, and, when any question of property arose, exhibited a knowledge at once minute and catholic. Watches, jewels, plate, seals, miniatures, notes, leases, horses, even books when they were in handsome bindings—nothing came amiss to a mind so truly enlarged.

“It is a pity,” Mr. Rudge observed one evening, while Wickham was lamenting, with much grace, the injustice of fortune, “that gentlemen’s friends so seldom know what is good for them.”

“My friends,” said Wickham, “have generally known very well what was good for themselves.”

“A common defect,” replied Mr. Rudge. “But families of great property are often careless. They leave value lying about, because they have never wanted the price of it. It is morally injurious to them.”

Wickham laughed. “You would restore them to virtue by relieving them of temptation?”

“I have known worse charities,” said Mr. Rudge.

There are suggestions which, had they been clothed in plain words, might startle even a practised conscience; but when they come dressed as pleasantry, and sit down among grievances, they are apt to be treated as old acquaintance. Wickham did not answer immediately. He stirred his glass, looked at the fire, and thought of Pemberley.

Pemberley had never been absent long from his imagination; but it had commonly appeared there as a scene of former mortification, a proof of Darcy’s ungenerous fortune, and a convenient ornament to resentment. Of late it assumed a more substantial form. He remembered not merely its woods and waters, which could not easily be put into a pocket, but cabinets, closets, plate-rooms, dressing-cases, little caskets upon tables, chains, seals, snuff-boxes, cameos, and miniatures—objects which rich people call trifles when they possess them, and heirlooms when they lose them. He had been bred in the neighbourhood of the house; he knew, or believed he knew, more of its habits than most who served in it; he had once moved there almost as a son, and afterwards as an enemy obliged to be civil. The knowledge was of a kind which, in a virtuous man, would have supplied recollection; in Mr. Wickham, it supplied ideas.

Lydia, too, thought often of Pemberley, though her thoughts had less arrangement. She had visited her sister there but once since Elizabeth’s marriage, and then not long; for Mrs. Darcy, while showing every proper kindness, had contrived that the visit should be short enough to preserve the comfort of the house and long enough to satisfy the claims of relationship. Lydia had arrived with bustle, admired loudly, examined freely, offended three servants by familiarity and two by command, declared the rooms monstrous handsome though rather grave, and departed with a conviction that Elizabeth had more finery than she knew how to enjoy.

Her memory, never accurate where gratitude was concerned, was singularly useful on the subject of ornaments. She recollected a little cabinet in Mrs. Darcy’s sitting-room, where the key was sometimes left attached by a ribbon; a set of pearl ornaments which Elizabeth had worn only in the evening and put aside with provoking indifference; a silver inkstand on Mr. Darcy’s table; a chain of old workmanship which had belonged, Lydia had been told, to Lady Anne; and a collection of small articles at Pemberley Cottage, the pretty retired house at the edge of the park, where the Darcys sometimes passed a day or two in greater privacy, and where Lydia had once been taken to luncheon and had found the china “sweetly odd” and the little drawing-room “quite fit for a bride.”

It is probable that Elizabeth, who had a just concern for propriety and a sisterly reluctance to suspect what she disliked, had not observed every movement of her guest. Lydia’s admiration had that active quality which brings the hands as well as the eyes into employment. A ribbon, a smelling-bottle, a trinket which had lain upon a work-table, and afterwards could not be perfectly accounted for, had on different occasions altered their residence; not, in Lydia’s phrase, from any thing one could call taking, but because she had “fancied them so prodigiously,” or “meant to ask Lizzy for them and forgot,” or “was sure nobody would miss such a little thing.” Such explanations had satisfied her at the time, and served afterwards to satisfy Mr. Wickham, who required very little persuasion to believe any property imperfectly guarded to be improperly retained.

One afternoon, when the rain had confined Lydia to her parlour and her spirits to a very unequal contest with the weather, she brought from a drawer a small brooch of antique setting. It was not handsome according to her taste, being of a severity which wanted sparkle; but its old gold and little dull stones had obtained for it, at the pawnbroker’s, more respect than she had expected. She had redeemed it that morning, not from affection, but because the man, with offensive penetration, had hinted that he should prefer not to see her again without the interest.

Wickham, entering while she held it, stopped short.

“Where did you get that?”

“La! how solemn you look. I have had it this age.”

“From Pemberley?”

“Well—perhaps. From that little cottage place of Lizzy’s. It was lying by some books, and I meant to tell her I had it, only she was so taken up with talking to Mr. Darcy about some tiresome tenants that I quite forgot. I dare say she never missed it. She has boxes full of such things.”

He took it from her and examined it with more seriousness than she thought so poor an ornament deserved.

“This is old,” said he.

“So the odious man said where I left it. He asked questions till I was ready to box his ears. I told him it was a family thing, which was true enough, for Lizzy is my sister.”

Wickham smiled slowly. “Yes. A family thing.”

“I could get very little upon it,” said Lydia, pouting. “Not near enough for Mrs. Ford’s subscription, and she made such a fuss. I am quite tired of people being shabby. If I had Lizzy’s jewel-box for one morning, I would shew them what it is to be well dressed.”

“One morning,” repeated Wickham.

He said no more then; but Lydia, who had quickness enough where her own vanity was engaged, perceived that he had become attentive.

After dinner—if the meal deserves the name, being composed chiefly of apology and potatoes—he drew his chair nearer to her and began to ask questions of Pemberley Cottage. At first she answered idly. The cottage was pretty, though dull; there were two parlours, or perhaps three; the windows looked upon trees; the housekeeper, or somebody like a housekeeper, had worn black silk; there was a plate closet near the passage, unless it was at the great house; Elizabeth had kept a writing-desk in the smaller room; Mr. Darcy had once unlocked a cabinet for a miniature; the servants went backwards and forwards because the family did not sleep there often; and nobody seemed in a hurry, which Lydia had always thought a sign that people valued themselves excessively.

Wickham’s questions were so judiciously intermingled with abuse of the Darcys, praise of Lydia’s observation, and occasional reference to their own hard case, that she grew animated by degrees. She remembered more. There was a side-door, used by servants from the gardens. There was a gravel path not seen from the road. Elizabeth had laughed—yes, actually laughed—when Lydia had lost herself between the little library and the still-room, and had said that Pemberley Cottage was not built to bewilder anybody. This recollection, being injurious to Elizabeth, fixed the geography much better in Lydia’s mind.

“She thought me a fool, I dare say,” cried Lydia. “Lizzy always did. Because I did not sit with a book in my hand and look sharp at everybody. But I saw more than she supposed.”

“I am convinced of it,” said Wickham.

“And I remember the pearls quite well. She wore them at dinner the day before we left—or was it the sapphires? At any rate, she put them into a case herself, and the maid carried it away, though why people must have maids for every thing I cannot imagine, except to make one feel poor. I am sure I could carry my own jewels if I had any.”

“And when the family are absent?”

“How should I know? I am not a housekeeper. But I know they are always going to one place or another—visiting Lady Catherine, or the Bingleys, or some great stupid people. Lizzy wrote to Jane last month that they were to be in town after Christmas; perhaps they are to leave the cottage shut up. I wish they would ask us. I should like to see whether she would stare at my green gown. It looks very well by candle-light.”

Mr. Wickham did not appear to have heard the last observation. “After Christmas,” he repeated. “In town.”

“Or before. I never remember dates. Jane would know, if one asked prettily.”

“Then you must ask prettily.”

Lydia clapped her hands. “Shall I write to dear Jane? I have been meaning to all day. Perhaps she may send something; and I shall put in about Lizzy’s movements quite naturally. I can say I long to know where everybody is, because we are so moped here. That will not look particular at all.”

It was one of Lydia’s misfortunes that, when she accidentally formed a prudent conclusion, she valued it only as a clever trick. She wrote accordingly to Jane that evening, in a style of great affection and little grammar, lamenting her solitude, inquiring after the children at Netherfield, asking whether Elizabeth was still at Pemberley or soon going south, and adding, as if the thought had just danced into her head, that she should dearly like to know whether Pemberley Cottage was used in winter, “for it was the sweetest little place, though Lizzy did not seem half sensible of it.”

Jane, who suspected no evil beyond imprudence, answered with her usual openness. Elizabeth and Mr. Darcy, she believed, were to leave Derbyshire for some weeks; there had been mention of a visit to town, and perhaps of a short stay with the Matlocks. Pemberley Cottage would be little used till spring, though some servants went there regularly to keep it in order. She hoped Lydia was well; she begged her to be cautious in her expenses; she enclosed a small sum, and wrote that Mr. Bingley joined in every kind wish.

This letter produced very different sensations in husband and wife. Lydia rejoiced over the enclosure, complained that small sums vanished the moment one changed them, and observed that Jane’s information was extremely stupid, though it was exactly what she had asked to receive. Wickham read the passage about Pemberley Cottage twice, then folded the letter and put it into his pocket.

“Why do you want Jane’s letter?” cried Lydia.

“To consider,” said he.

“Consider! You are always considering now. I had rather have something to wear.”

“You shall have something better than a new ribbon, if you are patient.”

Lydia, who had never willingly practised patience, was nevertheless pleased by the promise. It had the charm of being indefinite. Had Mr. Wickham named the object, she might have found fault with it; but “something better” filled her imagination with gowns, feathers, jewels, suppers, and a return of consequence. She asked many questions, which he answered by smiling; and there had always been something in his smile which persuaded her that he was cleverer than other men, though her experience had not yet proved that his cleverness was as profitable as it ought to be.

In the following days his absences became more frequent and his explanations more civil than exact. He had business with Blount, a call upon Skevington, an engagement with Rudge, a matter concerning a bill, a chance of advancement, a possibility, a certainty, a thing not yet to be named. All these phrases had, to Lydia’s ear, the sound of importance; and as they relieved her from hearing accounts, she approved them exceedingly. When money appeared, as it sometimes did, in small and irregular quantities, she asked no ungenerous questions. When it disappeared, as it always did with more punctuality, she lamented the dearness of every thing and wrote again to Jane.

Her society meanwhile sank another degree, though she described it as growing more entertaining. The ladies who had first admitted her because she was lively now avoided her because she was so; the distinction was not obvious to Lydia, but it was felt by them. Mrs. Ford’s parties, once an occasional indiscretion, became a habit; and habits are seldom so obliging as to remain at the level on which they were formed. There were suppers where the hour excused the company and the company disgraced the hour; card-tables where Lydia’s ignorance was encouraged until it had paid for the entertainment; and walks, taken under the protection of gentlemen whose civilities were so warm that a prudent woman would have found them chilly.

She was still flattered, and still believed flattery to be the most substantial proof of superiority. If a merchant’s clerk with curled hair told her she was born to be a duchess, she forgave him his hands; if a racing man praised her eyes in a manner less ceremonious than exact, she blushed, laughed, and repeated the compliment at home as evidence that she was admired by all ranks of life. There were invitations which she did not quite understand, offers of protection which seemed to her only another form of gallantry, and hints from women older in folly than herself that beauty, when properly conducted, need never be poor. Lydia heard, laughed, wondered a little, and was half offended only when it appeared that the admiration of some people expected gratitude of a kind not payable in smiles alone. She would not, she declared, be dictated to by anybody; and in this brave resolution she fancied herself virtuous, because obstinacy often wears to its owner the appearance of principle.

Wickham observed all this with feelings not easily to be praised. He was jealous when vanity required it, careless when interest recommended it, and indignant only when another man seemed likely to profit more than himself. His wife’s beauty, once his triumph, now appeared to him a resource imperfectly understood by her; and Lydia, who had never learned that a husband’s approbation may be more insulting than his censure, was gratified when he told her she might turn every head in Newcastle if she chose.

“I am sure I do choose,” said she. “Why should I not be admired? If Lizzy had half my spirits, she would not sit moping over her great house as if it were a chapel.”

“Elizabeth,” replied Wickham, “has the advantage of being admired by people who admire her possessions.”

“And I am admired for myself,” cried Lydia, delighted with the distinction.

“For the present,” said he, softly.

Had she been a woman of reflection, the words might have distressed her; but Lydia’s mind was mercifully protected against many pains by not admitting their meaning until it was too late. She drank her cordial, adjusted her bonnet, and went out to prove that admiration was still obtainable at a moderate expense.

The “tonics” had by this time become established members of the household. They stood, not indeed upon the sideboard with the candour of wine, but in cupboards, bandboxes, and drawers, from whence they were produced on occasions of fatigue, cold, disappointment, or anticipated pleasure. Lydia’s spirits, naturally abundant, acquired from them a brightness more sudden than lasting; and her subsequent languor was attributed to the cruelty of her situation, never to its supposed remedy. Wickham’s restoratives were taken in less domestic form, and generally among friends; but they returned with him in his eye, his voice, and that dangerous gaiety which makes a man agreeable to strangers and alarming to his family.

There were evenings when Lydia, after laughing too much abroad, cried a little at home. Her tears were seldom penitential; they were rather complaints in a liquid form. She wept because Jane was happy without deserving it more than other people; because Elizabeth had every thing and looked as if she had a right to it; because her father had not saved money; because her mother had no power to command it; because Wickham was unlucky; because she was admired and yet not rich; and because the world, after encouraging young women to marry officers, did so little for them afterwards. Wickham, if in good humour, soothed her; if in bad, left the room; and if in a speculative mood, used her discontent as bellows to his own.

“Do you know,” he said one night, when they sat again over the wretched fire which seemed to share their credit, “that there are persons who would say we have no claim upon Darcy?”

“Then they would be horrid wretches,” cried Lydia. “After all you suffered from him!”

“After all,” he repeated. “The living designed for me—my prospects blasted—my character traduced—my marriage made the subject of his interference—”

“Well, he did pay something then,” said Lydia, with a remnant of inconvenient memory. “Mamma said so.”

Mr. Wickham’s countenance clouded. “He paid what his pride made necessary. He purchased silence, and has been repaid in gratitude—by himself. A small expense, considering what he withheld.”

“I never can understand livings and all that,” said Lydia; “but I know Lizzy has more jewels than she can wear, and I have none fit to be seen. That is injustice enough for me.”

Wickham looked at her with approval. “Your reasoning is feminine, my dear, but not unsound.”

“I call it very sound. And if people will have quantities of things lying about, while their own sisters are pinched, they ought to be ashamed. I am sure, if I were mistress of Pemberley, I would give all my sisters something handsome.”

“You would be generous because you have been neglected.”

“Yes, I should. Though I dare say Kitty would tease me out of patience, and Mary would want books instead of lace. But Jane should have whatever she liked; and Lizzy—well, Lizzy would not want any thing, for I should not let her be richer than me.”

This speech, which united liberality and revenge in proportions very natural to its speaker, amused Wickham exceedingly. He drew from his pocket Jane’s letter, now creased by much consultation, and laid it on the table.

“Suppose,” said he, “that fortune were to put it in our power to correct a little of this inequality.”

Lydia opened her eyes. “How?”

“By availing ourselves of what is already, in some sense, ours.”

“I do not know what you mean, Wickham.”

“No. And perhaps it is better you should not know too much at once. But you have courage, Lydia.”

“To be sure I have.”

“And no foolish scruples about a family which has done little for you.”

“Scruples! I hate scruples. They are always in the way of something pleasant.”

He laughed, but with a gravity beneath it. “You remember Pemberley Cottage. You remember more than most people would suppose. If the Darcys are absent, if the servants are few, if certain articles lie neglected—articles whose value is useless to those who possess too much, and salvation to those who have been wronged—”

“Wickham!” cried Lydia, half rising, half delighted, half frightened, and wholly curious. “You cannot mean—”

“I mean nothing improper,” said he, with admirable composure. “Impropriety is a word invented by comfortable people to secure their comforts. I mean only that there are debts in this world which the law does not recognise, and restitutions which the timid misname.”

Lydia sat silent. It was not that her conscience spoke very loudly; it had never been encouraged to raise its voice, and was easily put out of countenance by stronger company. But the notion, dim as it yet was, had a newness which startled her. To borrow, to beg, to evade, to adorn necessity with pretty names—these were familiar arts; to pass beyond them required a moment’s education. Wickham, who understood the value of not hurrying a weak mind when vanity might lead it faster than argument, allowed her to sit with the thought.

At length she said, in a lower tone, “Lizzy would be in such a passion.”

“Elizabeth’s passions are always called principles,” replied he.

“And Mr. Darcy—”

“Would look grave. He has done so before, and the world survived it.”

“But if—if anybody knew?”

“Nobody need know what is managed with common sense. Besides, who would suspect us? We are poor; and poor relations are expected to be troublesome, not enterprising.”

Lydia giggled, though not quite at ease. “La! Wickham, how you talk. It sounds like a play.”

“Then let us hope,” said he, “that we have the better parts.”

From that evening the subject, though seldom named plainly, became the secret furniture of their thoughts. Lydia would break off in the midst of dressing to ask whether pearls were more easily altered than diamonds; whether old silver could be known again if melted; whether servants at great houses were very sharp; whether France was a pleasant country; and whether a woman with money enough might not pass for respectable anywhere. Wickham answered with varying degrees of caution, pleased by her curiosity when it served him, irritated when it exposed her folly, and always careful to mingle resentment with hope.

He saw Mr. Rudge more frequently. Mr. Rudge, on being told that a gentleman of family might have access to information respecting unused plate and jewels, expressed no surprise, which is one of the highest compliments such men can pay. He spoke of London—not the London of parks, routs, and respectable uncles, but of another city folded within it, where name, crest, and inheritance could be translated, with some loss indeed, into ready money; where old gold lost its memory, stones forgot their settings, and silver with a coat of arms became, after proper treatment, as modest as any other metal. He knew persons, he said, who asked no questions, which was untrue; they asked many, but not aloud.

Wickham returned from these conferences with a composure more animated than peace. Lydia, perceiving that something important approached, grew first vain, then restless, then indiscreet. She hinted to Mrs. Ford that she should not always be buried in Newcastle; told Captain Morris that some people would be surprised before long; and informed the thin lady in yellow that she had relations who might yet find out the danger of using her ill. These speeches, being understood by nobody, did little harm; and in circles where every one had some mystery of his own, a foolish woman’s mystery was merely an addition to the conversation.

At length a letter came from Mrs. Bennet, written in all the agitation of maternal consequence. It contained complaints of nerves, abuse of servants, raptures over Jane’s last baby, lamentations that Lydia never came home, and, buried among these familiar treasures, a piece of intelligence copied from Jane, who had received it from Elizabeth: Mr. and Mrs. Darcy were to leave Pemberley within a fortnight; the great house would remain fully staffed, but the Cottage would be shut up except for the usual attendance. Mrs. Bennet added that she thought it very odd Lizzy should go away when she had such a house to stay in, but rich people never knew what to do with themselves; and she hoped Lydia had a warm pelisse, for she dreamed twice of her catching cold.

Lydia read the letter aloud, laughing at her mother’s alarms and stopping only when Wickham held out his hand. He took the sheet, found the important lines, and his eyes rested upon them with an expression from which even Lydia could gather satisfaction.

“There,” cried she, eager to share the triumph without understanding its extent, “did I not say they were always gadding? I am sure, if I had Pemberley, I should never stir.”

“No,” said Wickham. “You would know its value.”

“I should wear the pearls every day, only to shew I could. Lizzy has no spirit.”

“She has security,” replied he; “and security makes people dull.”

Lydia came and leaned over his shoulder. “And are we to be secure, Wickham?”

He folded the letter carefully. “If courage and justice can make us so.”

“Justice!” She repeated the word with pleasure. It sounded much better than any plainer one, and had the advantage of being used by people in sermons, though not always, as Lydia conceived, to agreeable purpose. “Yes, I like justice. Lizzy ought to have justice. She has had every thing her own way much too long.”

Mr. Wickham kissed her hand with the gallantry which had once been so much admired in Meryton, and which still retained power over the lady most concerned to distrust it.

“My dear Lydia,” said he, “you were born for fortune.”

She believed him; and perhaps, in one sense, he was right. She had been born with all the dispositions which make fortune necessary and almost impossible: vanity without perseverance, courage without conduct, desires without delicacy, and spirits which could bear every thing except quiet. That such a woman should think herself neglected was not wonderful; that she should seek redress where she had never learned restraint was only the natural progress of a mind to which consequence had always appeared more desirable than character.

The price of being neglected was now nearly fixed in both their imaginations. It was to be paid not by amendment, which is a currency slow in circulation and little valued by the young, but by some brilliant alteration of circumstances, sudden enough to silence creditors and splendid enough to mortify relations. If Lydia still felt, at moments, a little flutter of alarm when Pemberley Cottage rose before her—not as a place of sweetness and hospitality, but as a house with doors, passages, keys, and cabinets—she found means to quiet it by remembering Elizabeth’s grave looks, Mr. Darcy’s pride, and the mortifying smallness of Jane’s last enclosure. Wickham, for his part, had long possessed that happy constitution of mind which can convert every temptation into an argument and every argument into a right.

Thus, while the Darcys pursued their arrangements in peaceful ignorance, and Jane rejoiced to think that her little kindnesses might still preserve a sister from severer distress, the Wickhams sat in their poor rooms in Newcastle and talked of fortune, justice, and injury, till the words grew so familiar that they lost all relation to truth. The fire smoked, the wind complained at the ill-fitted window, unpaid bills lay in a drawer with Lydia’s ribbons, and on the table between them Mrs. Bennet’s letter, creased and studied, seemed to promise a road out of poverty. That the road led downward did not much signify to persons who had long mistaken motion for escape.

## Chapter III  
### A Visit Not Paid

There is a species of preparation which, by being shared between two persons of very different understandings, acquires all the confidence of concert without any of its safety. Mr. Wickham planned because he had talents enough to be dangerous; Mrs. Wickham assisted because she had vanity enough to be led; and between his resentment and her folly there arose, in a very few days, an enterprise which neither would have dared to describe by its plainest name, though both could speak of it with great fluency under the titles of justice, necessity, and spirit.

It was fortunate for their purpose that Lydia had never been accustomed to examine words after they had once pleased her. “Restitution” had an air at once injured and respectable; “family claims” sounded extremely well; and “only taking what ought long ago to have been offered” was a phrase so full of comfort, that she repeated it till it became, in her own estimation, little less than a maxim. Had any person of common honesty asked her whether she meant to rob her sister, she would have been shocked at the vulgarity of the expression, and perhaps at the accusation; but to avail herself, in a season of hardship, of some part of that superfluity which Elizabeth enjoyed with so little animation, was a very different thing, and might be defended, she thought, by any body who knew the world.

Mr. Wickham encouraged these distinctions with all the tenderness of a husband and all the industry of an accomplice. He spoke much of his early wrongs, of Mr. Darcy’s pride, of the injustice of fortune, and of the indecency of allowing one branch of a family to glitter while another was suffered to fade. If at times Lydia’s courage drooped, it was revived by some skilful allusion to Elizabeth’s jewels, Jane’s carriage, or the mortifying probability that all her sisters would soon be pitying her. Pity, to Mrs. Wickham, had always been intolerable, unless accompanied by bank-notes; and the thought of being compassionated at Pemberley was more powerful than any scruple which Pemberley ought to have inspired.

The practical part of the matter was less agreeable to her. She preferred magnificence in the lump; details fatigued her, especially when they required recollection. Yet she was pleased to find her memory treated as a valuable article. Wickham questioned her on passages, windows, closets, bells, servants, hours, tables, cabinets, and the various contrivances by which the rich make imprisonment elegant and property troublesome to remove. Lydia, at first diverted by the importance given to her observations, grew impatient when she was expected to be exact; but resentment supplied what attention wanted. Every corner in which Elizabeth had once appeared easy, every chair in which Mr. Darcy had sat with his provoking composure, every drawer which had seemed to Lydia too well furnished for a younger sister’s comfort, returned to her mind with a clearness which virtue had never commanded from it.

Mr. Rudge was consulted; not in the presence of Mrs. Wickham, for Wickham still retained enough of gentlemanlike fastidiousness to dislike witnessing the whole degradation of his own proceedings, but afterwards, in hints and assurances, which she found more romantic than intelligible. Mr. Rudge, whose prudence consisted chiefly in anticipating the fears of other men, advised nothing in words that could be repeated. He only observed that daylight was the friend of boldness; that honest people seldom suspect an offence committed before dinner; that a parcel in a servant’s hand has more innocence than a jewel in a lady’s; and that family plate, when once separated from the sentiment attached to it, is mere metal with pretensions. Such reflections, delivered with great sobriety, had a wonderfully composing effect upon Mr. Wickham.

It was settled at length that they should quit Newcastle under the pretence of visiting an acquaintance at York; a pretence rendered the more secure by Lydia’s having so often spoken of visits which she never paid, that nobody now depended on her being where she said she was. Their landlady received the intelligence with a civility in which hope was scarcely disguised; and Lydia, offended by the woman’s satisfaction, declared as she packed that she had never been so ill-used by anybody whom she owed money.

Their wardrobes, though extensive in the number of useless articles, were ill adapted to concealment. Lydia had many gowns too showy to be respectable and too worn to be handsome; Wickham had coats which still knew how they ought to sit, though they had forgotten how they were to be paid for. The disguises therefore were procured elsewhere. Lydia was equipped in a plain dark gown, a close bonnet, and a shawl of that sober ugliness which recommends itself to no eye and is therefore one of the surest protections against curiosity. She protested at first with great warmth.

“I shall look like a housemaid,” cried she, viewing herself with disgust.

“My dear Lydia,” replied Wickham, “you will look like a woman whom no gentleman presumes to examine.”

“That is worse,” said Lydia. “I had rather be examined by every gentleman in Derbyshire.”

But when he represented to her that the very success of the design depended upon her appearing of less consequence than she was, and that she must endure a temporary eclipse in order to blaze afterwards in Paris, Bath, London, or wherever fortune might be most disposed to admire her, she was reconciled. To be shabby for an hour, on condition of being splendid for a year, was a bargain which even Lydia could comprehend; and she practised before the glass the air of a modest attendant, though the result was rather that of a young woman excessively angry at not being stared at.

Wickham’s own transformation was more successful. A rougher coat, a different hat, a little alteration in his hair, and an expression of civil obscurity made him appear what he had often pretended to be—a man engaged on business not quite his own. He had the great advantage, in all disguises, of knowing how gentlemen look when they are not wishing to be known; and if his countenance retained its beauty, he trusted that beauty, in persons supposed to be employed upon errands, would be set down to the accidents of nature and not to Wickham of the militia.

Their journey southward was performed with more silence than usually belonged to Mrs. Wickham. She was not without exhilaration; indeed the idea of unexpected fortune danced before her with its accustomed want of modesty; but there were intervals when the hedges, the inns, the changing horses, and the cold meals of the road seemed to be carrying her not merely towards Derbyshire, but towards some place in herself which she had no wish to visit. At such moments she was cross. She found fault with the tea, the roads, the curtains, the weather, Wickham’s gravity, and her own bonnet; and, having exhausted the external world, she returned, as she generally did, to Elizabeth.

“I dare say Lizzy is enjoying herself vastly in town,” said she, as they approached the country which she had once entered with so much noise and so little apprehension. “She will be going to plays, and seeing fine people, and thinking nothing of us.”

“Very probably,” said Wickham.

“And if she knew how poor we have been, she would only say it was all our own fault.”

“Undoubtedly.”

“That is so cruel! I am sure I never said any thing so unkind when she had nothing. She had only Longbourn then, as we all had; and I never grudged her my officers.”

“She has since learned to value distinctions.”

“She has learned to be proud,” cried Lydia. “And I cannot bear pride in one’s own sister. It is worse than in strangers.”

This settled the question for a considerable number of miles. To injure a proud person is, to some tempers, almost the same thing as to correct her; and Lydia, after having thus reformed the language of her enterprise, felt herself again nearly virtuous.

They reached the neighbourhood of Pemberley on a morning bright enough to make concealment seem absurd and therefore safe. The air had that clear winter sharpness which gives to every object a decided outline; the distant woods stood dark against a pale sky; and the great estate, with all its undisturbed consequence, spread before them in a manner very ill calculated to soothe those who were come to lessen it. Lydia, who had expected to feel only triumph, was disagreeably affected by the sight. She had entered Pemberley before as a sister, noisy, admired by herself, and confident of being forgiven every want of propriety because she was Mrs. Wickham. She approached it now as one who must not be recognised by the servants, must not pass the principal gate, must not laugh too loudly, must not even toss her head with freedom; and the change was mortifying in a degree which she did not choose to understand.

They did not go near the great house. Pemberley itself appeared, between plantations and rising ground, with a tranquil magnificence which made Lydia’s heart swell less with shame than indignation. It seemed to her very hard that any building should look so secure while she was so much disturbed. Wickham’s face, too, changed as he saw it. The smile by which he generally softened resentment was for a moment absent. He had once walked there with hopes which, though not honest, had been elegant; had imagined himself at ease in rooms where he was now known only as an error corrected; and though his principles were flexible, his vanity was constant. To be poor near Pemberley was an insult which he laid, as he had laid many things, at Darcy’s door.

The Cottage stood at some distance from the great house, sheltered by trees, and possessing that air of chosen retirement which is always particularly provoking to those who have never been able to retire from anything but their creditors. It was called a cottage because people of fortune are apt to give humble names to buildings which would astonish the industrious; and though smaller than Pemberley, it contained rooms enough for comfort, servants enough for negligence, and valuable objects enough to gratify a very extensive system of redress.

Their approach had been timed for an hour when the few persons attached to the place were dispersed by ordinary duties. Such arrangements, being the fruit of Mr. Wickham’s inquiries and Lydia’s recollections, need not be minutely exposed. It is sufficient that the habits of a house, once known, are often more open than its doors; and that servants, accustomed to see property respected, are not always prepared to defend it against those to whom gratitude would have been more becoming than curiosity. A note had preceded them, purporting to concern some articles to be examined and removed by order of authority; and if its authority was not of the most legitimate kind, it had the advantage of being written in a hand which looked as if it expected obedience. The world is full of people who believe a direction because it is troublesome to doubt it.

Wickham, with a confidence which had never deserted him except in the presence of creditors, presented himself as connected with necessary business from the estate. Lydia followed, carrying herself with what she intended for humble propriety, but which an attentive observer might have suspected of impatience for better clothes. The maid who admitted them was young, civil, and in some awe of any person who spoke as if he had a right to be obeyed. There was mention of the steward, of the absence of Mr. and Mrs. Darcy, of certain articles to be secured during the shutting up of the Cottage, and of an old inventory which required comparison. The phrases were excellent; they filled the air; and before the poor girl had quite determined whether she understood them, Wickham had passed the threshold.

Lydia, on finding herself within, experienced a flutter not wholly of pleasure. The hall was as she remembered it, perhaps prettier; the quiet parlour opened to the left; the little library beyond had the same sober shelves and low chair in which Elizabeth had once sat sewing while she talked to Mr. Darcy of some estate matter with a serenity Lydia had then called dullness. Nothing appeared alarmed by her presence. The pictures continued to look down; the clock moved with aristocratic calm; a vase of dried flowers stood where it had probably stood for weeks, neglected with all the elegance of wealth. Such composure was almost an affront.

“Make haste,” whispered Wickham, when the maid had been sent, by a civil contrivance, to seek a key not immediately necessary.

“I know that,” returned Lydia, in a tone which proved she had not known it till reminded. “Only it is very odd to be here again.”

“Oddities may be considered afterwards.”

He moved at once to the room which served, in the family’s absence, for the reception of smaller valuables removed from common use. Lydia followed, and the sight which met her restored her animation. There were cabinets, cases, drawers, and covered trays; not all unlocked, nor all containing treasure, but enough accessible to reward expectation and inflame it. Some plate had been left for the Cottage use; more had been collected there on account of repairs in another part of the estate, or for arrangement before the family’s return. There were salvers with arms which had eaten the bread of many generations; old spoons of curious shape; candlesticks heavy enough to suggest solidity of virtue to minds capable of such association; chased cups, little baskets, a tea-service, and several pieces whose antiquity had rendered them more important than their convenience.

Lydia’s eyes sparkled.

“Only see!” cried she, forgetting caution.

“Hush.”

“La! but Lizzy never uses half of these things. I am sure I never saw that little gold cup before. Why should it be shut up here doing nobody any good?”

“To prevent its benefiting those who want it,” said Wickham.

The answer was entirely to Lydia’s taste. She took up a silver box, admired the lid, opened it, found nothing in it, and was offended, as if the Darcys had practised a deception upon her personally. Wickham, more usefully employed, selected with an eye instructed by Mr. Rudge’s conversations. He knew enough to prefer small value in high concentration to bulky splendour, though he was not proof against the claims of old plate when convenience permitted. Lydia, meanwhile, fluttered from one object to another, now exclaiming at the prettiness of a chain, now despising a miniature, now discovering that a seal would make a charming trinket if it had not a stupid crest upon it.

It was some minutes before she found what her imagination had chiefly sought. In a cabinet, the key of which had been less carefully guarded than its contents deserved, lay several jewel-cases, placed there, as a memorandum attached indicated, for removal to town whenever Mrs. Darcy should send. Lydia seized upon the nearest with such eagerness that Wickham, for once, had to restrain not her fear but her rapture.

The first contained pearls—a set of great softness and lustre, which Elizabeth had worn with that quiet propriety by which handsome things are made to appear almost moral. Lydia remembered them instantly and hated them for having looked so well on her sister.

“These are the very ones!” she cried. “She wore them when I was there. I knew she did. And she said they were Lady Anne’s, as if that made them too good to be looked at. They would become me much better.”

“They will become us still better exchanged,” said Wickham.

“Must they be exchanged? I should like to keep them.”

“My dear Lydia, jewels so known cannot be worn by you in England.”

“Then I shall wear them in France.”

“Even France has eyes.”

This was vexatious; but another case diverted her. Diamonds, sapphires, old settings, brooches heavy with family consequence, rings, necklets, ornaments designed for hair, and those various costly trifles by which former ladies had contrived to be remembered, came forth one after another. Some were more valuable than beautiful; others had that union of both which makes even the ignorant pause. Lydia did pause. For a moment she was not merely greedy. The collected brightness before her, lying in its quiet cases, touched some feeling less triumphant and more painful. She saw Elizabeth’s hands placing them aside without haste; imagined Jane admiring them without envy; thought, with a short bitterness, how little she herself had ever possessed which did not soon pass into a drawer, a pledge, or a dispute. But the reflection, being unwelcome, was soon made useful.

“She has had too much,” said Lydia, as if answering an accusation.

“She has had what others earned for her,” replied Wickham.

“And I am her sister.”

“Precisely.”

There are tempers to which relationship is a claim only when it confers profit, and an annoyance when it demands duty. Mrs. Wickham, comforted by this excellent arrangement of domestic feeling, resumed her occupation.

The maid returned once, and was despatched again; an elderly woman was heard in a distant passage, complained of a draught, and was satisfied by hearing a gentlemanly voice mention Mr. Darcy’s orders in a tone which seemed to make inquiry impertinent. A boy came to the door with coals, stared at Lydia’s bonnet, and was sent away by Wickham with sixpence and a message. Daylight streamed in all the while; birds moved in the trees; somewhere in the house a clock struck; and the whole transaction proceeded with that terrible mildness by which wrongdoing, when not opposed, persuades the wrongdoer that it can hardly be wrong.

Parcels had been prepared—plain, serviceable, and unromantic. Lydia had expected, she scarcely knew what; perhaps a scene of breathless haste, perhaps a struggle with a lock, perhaps Wickham displaying those heroic qualities which in novels convert impropriety into adventure. Instead there was folding, wrapping, choosing, rejecting, listening, and carrying; a business-like degradation which gave little assistance to fancy. She began to be fatigued before she had done enough to justify fatigue.

“Is there much more?” said she.

“There is as much as prudence allows.”

“I thought fortune was to be a great deal more entertaining.”

“It will be entertaining afterwards.”

“I hope so. This gown scratches monstrously.”

Wickham, who could forgive her folly when it did not endanger him, and only then, gave her a look of impatience; but Lydia had already turned to a small writing-table in the adjoining room. It was Elizabeth’s. The polished wood, the orderly arrangement, the paper cut and folded, the pens set ready, the little seal with E. D. engraved upon it, the dried sprig laid between the leaves of a book—every thing expressed a mind at peace with its own possessions. Lydia opened a drawer. A few letters, tied and labelled, lay within; a ribbon, a pencil-case, a small enamel box, and a miniature of Jane, taken in the first year of her marriage. Lydia held it up.

“Dear Jane,” said she, with a sudden softness, “how pretty she looks! Not so pretty as she used to, perhaps; but then every body always said Jane was handsome.”

“Leave that,” said Wickham.

“I was not going to take it,” she answered, though she had not quite known that before he spoke. “It would be of no use.”

The distinction was perhaps not the noblest; but it saved the miniature. She put it back, not without a feeling which might have grown into shame had it been less hurried and more accustomed to encouragement. Her eye then fell upon a little case of coral ornaments, probably of small value compared with the rest, but lively in colour and very suitable to her taste. These she slipped into her pocket almost petulantly.

“Lizzy never cared for coral,” she said. “It is exactly the sort of thing she would despise.”

Thus satisfied that she was rescuing an ornament from neglect, she returned to the other room.

The most considerable difficulty arose not from locks, nor servants, nor conscience, but from abundance. Mr. Wickham, who had often wanted five pounds with a desperation which made the sum sublime, now found himself embarrassed by wealth in inconvenient forms. A great branch of silver, magnificent and useless for immediate flight, had to be abandoned; two heavy dishes, though dear to his resentment, were judged too troublesome; a jewel-case likely to be recognised by its shape was emptied and left; a little strong-box, after much examination, was found to contain papers whose value, being legal and not portable, excited his disgust. Lydia, on the contrary, wished to take every thing which shone, and several things which did not, because they looked as if they might shine after cleaning.

“You do not know the worth,” said Wickham, in a suppressed voice.

“I know that more is better than less.”

“Not when more prevents escape.”

“I hate always thinking of escape. It sounds so low.”

“Then think of triumph.”

This restored her. She assisted again, though her assistance consisted chiefly in laying objects in the wrong parcels and asking whether each would buy a carriage. Wickham’s answer became at last no more than a look; and Lydia, affronted by his want of gallantry at so important a moment, consoled herself by deciding that when they were rich she would make him very uneasy if he ever spoke sharply to her.

At length the work was done. The parcels, innocent in appearance, had acquired a weight far beyond their looks. Plate was wrapped in linen; jewels were concealed where no casual glance would find them; smaller articles of gold and enamel, seals, chains, snuff-boxes, and miniatures in jewelled frames, were disposed with a skill which Lydia admired the more because she did not understand it. Some few objects she had secreted separately for herself, partly from fear that Wickham would sell every thing, partly from the natural pleasure of possessing something he did not know of. A marriage founded upon imprudence does not always improve into confidence when enriched by theft.

Before they quitted the principal room, Lydia looked back. The disorder was not great. Empty cases, some displaced covers, a drawer left open, gaps where silver had stood—these were all; yet to any one who loved the place, they would have spoken loudly enough. To Lydia they said only that Lizzy would be vexed. The thought gave her a thrill so near delight that it frightened her by its violence.

“She will be in such a taking,” she whispered.

Wickham smiled. “She will have the consolation of being right after the event. It is one of her favourite pleasures.”

“And Mr. Darcy?”

“He will be silent. Rich men are often silent when they cannot immediately hang somebody.”

“Do not talk of hanging,” cried Lydia, turning pale.

“My love, I talk of his wishes, not his power.”

This was not very soothing; but Wickham took her arm with decision, and they passed once more into the hall. The young maid, still uncertain whether she ought to feel important or alarmed, was informed that certain articles had been removed for security, that more directions would arrive from the steward, and that she had behaved with great propriety. Praise from a handsome man, even when disguised, is seldom thrown away. She curtseyed, blushed, and wished them good morning.

They left by the side entrance, in the full composure of noon. No storm favoured them; no darkness concealed them; no dramatic consequence marked the instant. A gardener at a distance touched his hat; a cart moved slowly along a lower path; the rooks made a great noise over affairs probably more innocent; and Pemberley Cottage, robbed in broad light by persons received under the shadow of family connexion, resumed its quiet aspect as soon as they had passed from view.

Lydia did not breathe freely till the trees hid the house.

“Now!” she exclaimed, half laughing, half trembling. “Now we have done it.”

“Not loudly.”

“I cannot help it. Oh, Wickham, are we prodigiously rich?”

“If Mr. Rudge’s judgement is good, and my memory does not deceive me, richer than many who will continue to call us poor.”

“How much? Tell me directly.”

“Enough.”

“I hate enough. It is what people say when they mean to be disagreeable. Is it ten thousand? Twenty? Fifty?”

“Possibly more.”

“More!” Lydia stopped short, to the great inconvenience of their pace. “More than fifty thousand pounds?”

“In value—if properly managed—something approaching twice that sum is not impossible.”

The words did what no moral argument could have done: they struck her dumb. One hundred thousand pounds, or any sum in its neighbourhood, was not to Lydia a number but a climate. It contained gowns beyond calculation, jewels without end, carriages, suppers, opera-boxes, revenge upon relations, admiration from strangers, and the delightful power of refusing those who had once refused her. That such wealth, reduced by the necessities of concealment, fear, and dishonest brokerage, would shrink under every hand through which it passed, was a truth which Wickham imperfectly knew and Lydia could not have endured. To her, at that moment, they had taken possession not merely of property, but of fate.

“A hundred thousand pounds!” she repeated, when speech returned. “Then I shall have diamonds of my own.”

“You shall have whatever prudence permits.”

“Prudence again! I do believe prudence is the most tiresome word in the language. But I shall never be poor any more; that is one comfort. Jane will be astonished. Lizzy will not know what to say.”

“Let us be careful that she has nobody to say it to on our account.”

This severity checked her for perhaps half a minute. Then the image of Elizabeth’s astonishment proved irresistible.

“I wish I could see her face when she misses the pearls.”

“I had rather be in London,” said Wickham.

The remainder of their escape from the neighbourhood was accomplished without interruption. It is not necessary to follow them through every change of conveyance, every anxious look, every false name, and every shabby precaution by which guilt endeavours to imitate respectability. They passed as unremarked as thousands pass daily who have nothing to conceal, and derived from that very circumstance a confidence which neither innocence nor wisdom had given them. At an inn, Lydia found leisure to complain of the dinner; at another, Wickham examined the parcels with an attention which made her uneasy; and before evening they were many miles from Pemberley, carrying with them enough of its ancient splendour to ruin several consciences, had consciences been articles in their possession.

It was late before the first discovery was made at the Cottage. A drawer, which ought to have held a case, did not hold it; a tray, remembered in the morning, was not to be found at night; the young maid, questioned, grew first confused and then terrified; the elderly servant declared she had misliked the business from the beginning, though she had said nothing of the kind at the time; the steward was sent for; messages flew to the great house; and by degrees that quiet consternation spread through the household which is more painful, among well-regulated people, than louder distress. The loss, at first supposed considerable, became, upon examination, alarming; upon further examination, almost incredible. Names of former Darcys, of Lady Anne, of old family settlements, of wedding gifts, christening cups, miniatures, jewels worn at courts and preserved in cabinets, were mingled with questions of locks, servants, keys, and orders. It was a violation not merely of property but of memory.

Mr. and Mrs. Darcy were absent, and perhaps it was mercy that they were so. There are moments in which even the most affectionate haste cannot restore what has been taken; and Elizabeth, had she stood that evening in her little room and seen the spaces where familiar objects had lain, must have felt a pang less for jewels than for the hand by which she would too soon suspect them removed. For there are injuries which a stranger may commit and leave only anger behind; a relation, in committing them, adds humiliation.

Of the value of the stolen articles various reports were afterwards spread, as is usual when society has the pleasure of being shocked. Some called it fifty thousand pounds, that they might be believed; others named a hundred, that they might be heard; and those who knew most allowed that, in jewels, plate, antiquities, and family pieces not easily estimated by any market, the loss approached a sum which common prudence would have blushed to imagine lying within reach of such hands. Mr. Rudge, when the account reached him in its more useful form, was of opinion that the acquisition, if divided between fear and necessity with proper expedition, might yet make two imprudent people magnificently uneasy for the rest of their lives.

The Wickhams, however, had not yet arrived at uneasiness. Lydia, seated in a private room on the road to London, with a poor fire before her and treasure in parcels about her, was in spirits almost equal to those of her first elopement. That earlier adventure had promised only a husband and a scandal; this offered, besides scandal, the means of paying for it. She took out the coral ornaments she had concealed, held them against her gown, and laughed to see how well they became her even in disguise.

“I am sure,” said she, “if this be being ruined, it is vastly more agreeable than being respectable in Newcastle.”

Mr. Wickham, who was counting, estimating, and already suffering under the first anxieties of possession, did not immediately answer.

“Do not look so grave,” she continued. “You used to be so charming when we had nothing. One would think a fortune had made you dull.”

“A fortune, my dear Lydia, is a delicate thing till it is safe.”

“Then let us make it safe directly. I should like to go to London to-morrow, order a pelisse, and drive in the Park. I do not see why we should hide, now we are rich.”

“Because,” said he, with more truth than tenderness, “we are rich in a manner not generally approved.”

Lydia pouted. “There you are, calling it by disagreeable names after persuading me it was justice.”

“It is justice,” replied Wickham, recovering his tone; “but the world is slow in recognising justice when it is not done by lawyers.”

This explanation satisfied her. She had no great opinion of lawyers, except when they were employed against other people; and she was willing to believe that the world, which had never properly admired her marriage, might be equally mistaken about her fortune. She leaned back, wearied, excited, and flushed by more than triumph, while Wickham secured the parcels once more and spoke of London, Mr. Rudge, foreign passages, and the necessity of discretion.

Outside, the road was dark; the inn creaked in the wind; a servant laughed in the passage; horses stamped in the yard. Nothing in the scene was splendid, and yet Lydia’s imagination filled it with future brilliance. She saw herself no longer slighted, no longer petitioning Jane, no longer enduring Elizabeth’s advice; she saw gowns, jewels, rooms, servants, introductions, all the ornaments of consequence without any of its duties. That the money which was to purchase these pleasures had been taken from the very house where she had once been received with kindness did not greatly disturb her. Gratitude, in minds of her construction, is apt to sleep soundly unless awakened by immediate advantage; and resentment, being a much more active creature, kept watch beside it.

Thus ended the visit which had not been paid. They had entered as shadows of necessity and departed as possessors of wealth; they had carried away silver which had brightened innocent tables, jewels which had adorned honourable women, and heirlooms whose value lay half in gold and half in remembrance. Their success was complete; and success, to persons unaccustomed to be checked by principle, has all the appearance of justification. It remained only to convert Pemberley into money, and money into safety—a process in which many discover, too late, that stolen prosperity is the most expensive article in the world.

## Chapter IV  
### London Reckonings

London, which receives all sorts of people with a civility proportioned rather to their purses than to their principles, made no objection to Mr. and Mrs. Wickham’s arrival. It had long been accustomed to the entrance of persons who wished to be forgotten in one quarter and admired in another; and if conscience sometimes follows such travellers to town, she is generally obliged, as has been observed, to take very inconvenient lodgings, and is seldom admitted to the principal apartment.

The Wickhams reached it with tempers very differently disposed. Lydia was all expectation; Wickham all calculation. She saw already the shop-windows brightening in her honour, mantua-makers awakened to a proper sense of their vocation, milliners throwing aside inferior customers, and every street conducting her either to pleasure or revenge. He saw danger in hackney-coaches, informers in doorways, curiosity in servants, and deduction in every tradesman who looked too long at a parcel. A fortune acquired in the common way may be enjoyed with composure by those who know how it came; but a fortune which has changed its owner without the ceremony of consent is apt to be troublesome at first, and requires, like certain ill-bred acquaintance, to be introduced under another name before it can be received in good company.

They did not go to any hotel of fashion. Lydia, who had imagined that one of the first uses of wealth was to be seen with it, was much offended by this prudence, and declared that nothing could be more provoking than to have riches and be obliged to behave like poor people.

“My dear Lydia,” said Wickham, whose tenderness always improved when it cost nothing, “a little obscurity now will purchase éclat hereafter.”

“I hate obscurity,” cried she. “It is only another word for nobody’s looking at one. I am sure, when I was at Brighton, I never thought of being obscure.”

“And perhaps Brighton had its share in teaching us the value of concealment.”

This was a reflection too near the truth to be agreeable; and Lydia, who had no taste for recollections unless they proved her consequence, turned to the window of their hired lodging in a narrow street not far enough westward to be genteel, nor eastward enough to be cheap, and lamented that London should contain so many disagreeable houses when it also contained so many fine ones.

Their rooms were respectable only in the sense in which many things are respectable—by being neither very dirty nor very new. The chairs had an air of having supported many hopes which had not prospered; the curtains had once been crimson, and retained, like some faded beauties, the recollection rather than the evidence of colour; and the looking-glass, though spotted and somewhat ill-natured, was still capable of returning to Lydia a face which she considered, under all circumstances, a sufficient apology for imprudence.

The parcels were placed in the inner room, and for the first night Wickham scarcely slept. Lydia slept extremely well, having tried on, in her fancy, several gowns which were not yet made, and having brought Elizabeth, with great satisfaction, into every scene where her own magnificence was to be displayed. Wickham, meanwhile, listened to wheels, steps, voices, and the watchman’s cry, with an attention which proved that fear can sometimes give industry to minds otherwise little acquainted with it. At every sound he thought of Pemberley; at every silence he thought of Mr. Darcy. It is possible he thought of justice; but, if so, he had the discretion not to mention it.

Mr. Rudge waited upon them the next morning. He came at an hour when honest business is commonly awake, though his own was seldom the better for daylight. He was a small, dry man, with a face which seemed to have been made by prudence and pinched afterwards by suspicion. His dress was sober, his voice low, and his manner so very cautious that one might almost have believed him virtuous, had his acquaintance been different. He congratulated Mr. Wickham without warmth, bowed to Mrs. Wickham without looking at her, and requested, before any particulars were entered upon, that the servant might be sent away, the door locked, the shutters somewhat closed, and every unnecessary expression avoided.

Lydia, who had expected a person of Mr. Rudge’s profession to be more entertaining, was soon disgusted.

“La! Mr. Rudge,” said she, “you speak as if we were in a church.”

“I have known less danger in churches, ma’am,” replied he, “than in rooms where people talk too freely.”

“I am sure I never talk too freely,” said Lydia, with all the sincerity of one who had never known what freedom of speech might cost.

Mr. Rudge allowed the assertion to pass, as men of his habits often do with the more innocent falsehoods of society, and applied himself to the parcels. At the first appearance of the jewels his eyes, which had hitherto seemed made only for narrowing, opened with an expression approaching to admiration. The pearls, the diamonds, the old brooches, the rings with initials and dates, the miniature frames surrounded by brilliants, and the ornaments of hair-work and enamel, drawn forth from their coverings, produced in him that grave sensibility which valuable property will sometimes excite in persons proof against every softer appeal.

“Family pieces,” said he, after a pause.

“Very much so,” answered Wickham.

“Too much so.”

Lydia did not understand him, and therefore disliked him the more.

“They are very handsome,” cried she. “That pearl necklace becomes me prodigiously. I wore it last night, only for a minute, before Mr. Wickham snatched it away as if I had been going to walk down Bond Street in it.”

Mr. Rudge looked at her then, and the look was not flattering. “No lady,” said he, “should wear what she has not leisure to explain.”

“I could explain it very well. I should say it was mine.”

“That, ma’am, is sometimes the worst explanation.”

Wickham smiled, but Lydia felt herself ill-used. She had never been fond of advice when it came without admiration, and Mr. Rudge’s civility had nothing in it to console vanity.

The examination proceeded. Some articles, he declared, must not be sold entire upon any account. Certain stones must be taken from their settings; arms must be effaced; inscriptions destroyed; pairs divided; old work, though valuable to collectors, must sometimes be sacrificed to safety; and plate, however noble in appearance, would in many instances be worth more when it had ceased to remember whose table it had adorned. Lydia heard all this with pouting incredulity.

“What is the use,” said she, “of having beautiful things, if nobody is to know they are beautiful?”

“The use, ma’am,” returned Mr. Rudge, “is to get money for them before anybody knows they are missed.”

“But they are missed by this time,” said Lydia, with a little laugh which had more sharpness than mirth. “I dare say Lizzy is in a monstrous fright.”

Mr. Rudge’s hand stopped upon a case of diamonds. “Mrs. Darcy,” said he, “is not the person whose fright I should chiefly consider.”

There was something in his manner which for an instant checked even Lydia. Wickham, who perfectly understood that fear had now become a partner in their fortune, asked where and how the first exchanges might be made. Mr. Rudge, after looking again towards the door, named no names at first, then half names, then streets by description, and shops by some peculiarity of window, entrance, or sign. There was a man near Holborn who dealt in broken gold as innocently as if it had been born in fragments; a foreigner in a court off the Strand who knew the history of no jewel after it came into his hand; a woman, said to be the widow of a naval officer by those who had never inquired into either husband or commission, who could convey small valuables abroad with a tenderness for them quite maternal; and certain warehouses near the river, where silver, when once deprived of its crest, became as humble and as marketable as any other metal.

The names, when at last they were spoken, had nothing romantic in them. Lydia was disappointed. She had expected, from so much mystery, persons in cloaks, rooms hung with dark velvet, perhaps a little danger of the sort which makes a heroine interesting without injuring her bonnet. Instead, she heard of Mr. Clegg, Mr. Barrow, old Levy, Mrs. Platt, and a young man with red hair who was not to be trusted unless watched by somebody less dishonest than himself. Wickedness, brought close, is often as vulgar as poverty; and Lydia, whose imagination preferred vice when well dressed, was vexed to find that her fortune must pass through hands which would have been refused at any assembly she cared to attend.

During the next days, Wickham was much abroad. He went out early and returned late; sometimes elated, sometimes sullen, always more guarded than Lydia liked, and never bringing back so much money as she had expected. Every thing, it appeared, lost value by being sold in haste; jewels were depreciated by being known, plate by being old, old work by being unfashionable, fashion by being dangerous, and danger by being expensive. Mr. Rudge, whose services were of the utmost consequence and whose conscience was not in the way, took care that his prudence should be properly acknowledged. Mr. Clegg found flaws in stones which had glittered unblemished for three generations; Mr. Barrow shook his head over silver which had reflected many respectable dinners; old Levy declared that nothing was so uncertain as pearls, unless it were the people who brought them; and Mrs. Platt, with tears in her eyes and a bonnet of extreme sensibility, accepted a packet of small jewels at a price which proved how deeply she felt the risk.

Wickham submitted because he must. There are humiliations peculiar to dishonest gain. A man who has injured his benefactor may still fancy himself superior to the tradesman who cheats him afterwards; but the superiority gives little comfort when it cannot be turned into cash. He had imagined himself master of a great treasure; he discovered himself dependent on every inferior rogue who had the means of disposing of it. A crest which had once been an ornament became an inconvenience; an inscription recording affection, birth, or marriage was no longer tender, but dangerous; the very antiquity which made an article precious to its possessors made it troublesome to sell; and family pride, after passing through Mr. Darcy’s cabinets, Mr. Wickham’s hands, and Mr. Rudge’s precautions, ended at last in a furnace, a file, or a bargain made in a back room smelling of tobacco.

Lydia knew less of this process than she wished, and more than Wickham intended. He did not choose to admit her to all his consultations; she did not choose to be excluded from any thing which concerned her splendour. Their disputes increased accordingly. She accused him of concealment; he accused her of imprudence; she reminded him that without her knowledge of Pemberley nothing could have been done; he answered that without his conduct they would both have been in custody before the second parcel was opened. Each was right enough to be angry, and wrong enough to be unreasonable; and thus domestic harmony, never very firmly established between them, now discovered that wealth could provide new materials for old tempers.

Still, money came. Not the imagined hundred thousand pounds—that delightful climate of Lydia’s fancy—but sums sufficiently large to intoxicate persons who had lately been unable to pay for coal without complaint. Bank-notes lay upon the table; gold was counted; jewels became drafts; silver became packets; packets became promises; promises, after much diminution, became money again. The total, though grievously reduced by the modest necessities of rogues, was yet such as might have satisfied many people whose wishes had been trained by honesty. It did not satisfy Lydia, because she had begun with expectation; and expectation, like a bad servant, consumes more than it earns.

She would be dressed. Upon this point Wickham’s authority, Mr. Rudge’s warnings, and common sense itself were equally unsuccessful. A woman who has endured shabby gowns in the belief that fortune is approaching seldom receives fortune with a becoming preference for economy. Lydia had felt the scratches of disguise, the insults of plainness, and the sorrow of being unobserved; she considered herself entitled to some immediate compensation. A mantua-maker was summoned—discreetly, Wickham insisted; expensively, Lydia contrived. There were muslins, silks, ribbons, feathers, gloves, and a pelisse of a colour which she declared no eye could resist. Wickham objected to the colour precisely for that reason.

“Must every thing I wear be chosen to make me look like nobody?” cried she. “I might as well have stayed in Newcastle.”

“You may be observed too much.”

“And what is the use of being handsome if one is not observed? I am sure Lizzy never understood that. She always liked to look as if she did not care how she looked, because she had all the jewels and could afford to be careless.”

“My dear, Mrs. Darcy’s manner is not our present business.”

“It is always our business,” said Lydia, with more truth than she knew. “She is the reason of every thing.”

Wickham did not deny it. It was an arrangement of causes which suited him as well as her. To make another’s prosperity responsible for one’s own misconduct is one of the most convenient economies of conscience.

Lydia’s new gowns arrived; and for a few days she was happy after her fashion. She dressed at improper hours, changed without occasion, and consulted the glass as if it had been a committee appointed to restore her consequence. The spotted mirror of their lodging, though unequal to the duties imposed upon it, gave back enough to encourage her. She was still young; her complexion, assisted by excitement and by certain lively cordials which London supplied with less inquiry than Newcastle, could still impose upon candles; her eyes had animation, if not repose; and her figure, though altered by restless habits and careless hours, retained that air of youth which had once been mistaken for charm by many officers and by herself for power.

She went out also, more than prudence allowed. At first in hired carriages with the blinds half drawn; then to shops where a lady might be seen without being known; then, when caution had grown tiresome, to places where being unknown was almost as agreeable as being admired. London abounds in societies which ask few questions, provided the answer to the first be paid in ready money. Lydia found women who praised her pelisse, men who bowed too low, rooms where music was loud enough to silence reflection, and tables where the refreshments had a spirit in them not wholly derived from hospitality. She was delighted. To be complimented by people whom she would once have despised was not, indeed, perfect triumph; but admiration, like some other articles of inferior quality, may be very acceptable when one has been long without it.

Wickham frequented his own assemblies—gentlemen’s society of dubious vigour and repute, where cards were less an amusement than a transfer, and where the talk, though animated, seldom improved the reputation of any absent person. He was handsome still; he could still smile, still relate, still seem injured with grace, still make inferiors feel flattered by his condescension and equals by his confidence. For a week he succeeded in appearing prosperous without explaining prosperity, and unfortunate only in the elegant degree which invites sympathy. But the habit of gaming, which had never quitted him from principle, returned with the ease of an old acquaintance; and money which had been obtained at such hazard began to depart with a freedom which would have been astonishing had any thing in Mr. Wickham’s life given reason to expect retention.

Lydia suspected this before she knew it. She had been careless of money when there was none; she became watchful the moment there was enough for another person to waste. It is a common improvement in economy. She found that drawers were locked which had not been locked before; that Wickham’s explanations were longer than his absences required; that bank-notes, once seen, disappeared into a mystery called business; and that whenever she asked what remained, he answered by asking what she had spent.

Their quarrels, though not loud enough to disturb the whole house, were sufficiently lively to gratify the maid who brought up the tea. Lydia cried that he was using her fortune; Wickham replied that her fortune would have been a coral necklace and a constable without him. She accused him of caring for nobody but himself; he observed, with perfect composure, that her discovery came late for a wife of two years’ standing. She declared she would write to Jane; he advised her, if she wished to make the intelligence general, to write also to the newspapers. This silenced her; for though Lydia had never been fond of secrecy, she had began to understand that there are occasions on which publicity may be carried too far.

The newspapers, indeed, soon became a source of considerable disturbance. The account of the robbery at Pemberley Cottage appeared first in cautious terms, then in enlarged ones, and finally with that mixture of fact and ornament which enables the public to be at once informed and entertained. “A daring depredation,” “a most extraordinary breach of confidence,” “property of immense value,” “family jewels,” “ancient plate,” “persons supposed acquainted with the domestic arrangements of the estate”—these phrases met Wickham’s eye at breakfast and made the coffee disagree with him. A reward was mentioned; inquiries were proceeding; officers had been sent to London; several suspicious circumstances had been traced.

Lydia read the account after him, and was at first more offended than alarmed.

“Daring depredation!” cried she. “How vulgar! I am sure there was nothing daring in it, except my wearing that odious bonnet. And ‘persons acquainted with the domestic arrangements’—that is as much as to say they think it was somebody genteel.”

“It says quite enough,” answered Wickham, taking the paper from her.

“And ‘breach of confidence’! I hate such expressions. Lizzy will make the worst of every thing, I dare say.”

“Mrs. Darcy will not need to make it worse.”

There was a tone in his voice which displeased and frightened her. She took back the paper when he had thrown it aside, and read again, more slowly. The words began to lose their absurdity. “Family jewels”—she saw the pearls. “Ancient plate”—she saw the silver branches they had been obliged to leave and the dishes they had not. “Domestic arrangements”—she saw herself in the quiet room at the Cottage, opening Elizabeth’s drawer. “Breach of confidence”—she saw Jane’s miniature looking up from among folded letters.

For the first time since their success, her pleasure suffered a check not caused by fear alone. It was not remorse in any stable or respectable form; Lydia’s mind had not been prepared by exercise for so strong a sentiment. But an uneasiness, small and cold, like air entering through a crack in a warm room, found its way in. She was angry at feeling it, and therefore angry with Elizabeth.

“I dare say,” said she, “Lizzy is sitting as composed as possible, and making Darcy admire how well she bears it. She always did like to be better than other people in a quiet way.”

“Would you prefer her to be in hysterics?”

“I should prefer her to have less to lose,” cried Lydia.

This, being sincere, relieved her; and she dressed herself with more than usual care, as if a becoming hat could refute an accusation.

Yet that night she slept ill. The cordials which had lately supplied her spirits with a promptness superior to nature did not bring rest in proportion. She dreamed confusedly of Pemberley and Longbourn, of silver melted into water and pearls rolling away under doors, of Mr. Darcy standing without speaking in a passage which grew longer as she tried to pass him, and of Elizabeth’s eyes fixed upon her, not with anger, but with a sorrowful comprehension more mortifying than reproach. Lydia had always preferred blame to understanding; blame could be answered, denied, laughed at, or resented; understanding left one nothing to do but be seen.

She woke displeased, fatigued, and cold, though the fire had not gone out. Wickham was not in the room. On the table lay one of her new gloves, a newspaper folded to the account from Derbyshire, and a little enamel box which she had taken from Elizabeth’s writing-table almost without knowing why. She opened it. It contained nothing but a narrow ribbon and a slip of paper in Elizabeth’s hand, some memorandum of household use, plain, orderly, insignificant. The writing was entirely Elizabeth—firm without flourish, clear without effort. Lydia shut the box hastily.

“La! what nonsense,” she said aloud, though nobody had accused her.

But she did not put the box among the things to be sold.

A letter from Jane would have been the severest punishment which could then have reached her, and, by good fortune or good sense, none came. Jane knew nothing certainly; Elizabeth perhaps suspected too much to speak; Mr. Darcy, it may be believed, acted rather than wrote. Their silence, however, did not comfort Lydia as much as their reproaches might have done. She had long been accustomed to interpret advice as ill-nature, assistance as obligation, and refusal as cruelty; but silence was less manageable. It left her to furnish the meaning herself, and her imagination, though never well regulated, could be troublesome when fear directed it.

Wickham perceived her alteration, and was not pleased by it. A Lydia uneasy from vanity, debt, or jealousy was a companion he understood; a Lydia visited by intervals of recollection was inconvenient. He had married her folly, not her conscience, and considered any improvement in the latter as a breach of the original contract. He took pains, therefore, to restore her spirits by all the arguments to which she had formerly been most sensible. He spoke of France, of dress, of gaiety, of people who knew nothing of the Darcys and cared less; of a life where they would be judged by their expenditure rather than their connections; of Paris, where, according to his representation, a handsome woman with money might be forgiven almost any thing except dullness.

“Paris!” cried Lydia, catching at the word. “Shall we really go to Paris?”

“To France, certainly; to Paris, very probably, when it is prudent.”

“And I shall have French gowns?”

“You shall have every thing French ingenuity can devise, if English curiosity will give us time to secure it.”

“I always said England was a horrid place when one is not admired. But Jane—” She stopped.

“What of Jane?”

“I do not know. She would look so shocked if she knew. Not angry, perhaps; Jane never looks angry enough. But shocked.”

“My dear Lydia, Mrs. Bingley is amiable by profession. She would be shocked with the utmost sweetness, and forgive with the utmost inconvenience to ourselves.”

Lydia laughed faintly. “Dear Jane! She would forgive, I believe. Only she would cry.”

“And you never liked tears unless they were producing a present.”

This was said lightly, and Lydia laughed again; but the laugh did not last. “She was always kind to me,” said she, with a little sullenness, as if kindness had been an impertinence.

“Then let us not distress her by remaining where discovery might oblige her to acknowledge the connexion.”

The argument was excellent, because it made flight appear considerate. Lydia willingly adopted it. To leave England, not because they were pursued, but because Jane might be distressed by their being found, gave a delicacy to the scheme which pleased her almost as much as the thought of French lace.

Preparations were consequently begun. Wickham, now thoroughly convinced that England had become ungrateful and unsafe, urged expedition. The inquiries in London had drawn nearer than he liked. One of Mr. Rudge’s acquaintances had disappeared for two days and returned with a face too pale for innocence; Mr. Clegg had refused a second interview except upon terms of monstrous caution; a servant in their lodging had shown an interest in the newspaper; and a man whom Wickham believed he had seen near Pemberley crossed him in the Strand with an air of not seeing him at all. These circumstances, though singly capable of explanation, made together a society in which he did not wish to remain.

Their property, now reduced to bank-notes, bills, foreign drafts, a few portable jewels chosen less for beauty than concealment, and a quantity of gold inconveniently heavy for persons who must appear unencumbered, was divided with much suspicion and little tenderness. Wickham insisted on keeping the principal sum; Lydia insisted on keeping something which he could not touch. She remembered the coral ornaments and applauded her own foresight. He allowed her a packet of notes, smaller than she supposed due to her, larger than prudence approved; she hid two rings and the enamel box among her linen; he hid several things where she would not have looked, which is perhaps no great praise of the hiding-place.

Mr. Rudge advised a passage from Dover under names which had no connexion with either Wickham or Bennet, and displayed in this advice as much solicitude for their safety as was consistent with having been paid. He parted from them with grave compliments, assurances that nothing more could be done with advantage for the present, and a recommendation that they should write to nobody, trust nobody, and remember nobody.

“I am sure I shall be very glad to remember nobody in England,” said Lydia, “except perhaps Jane, and Colonel Forster’s wife, and two or three officers, and my mother if she were not so tiresome about nerves.”

Mr. Rudge bowed as if this were a catalogue of the utmost political importance, and withdrew.

The last evening in London was not happy. Lydia had proposed a visit to the theatre, as a proper farewell to a country which had behaved ill to her; Wickham refused with a decision she thought extremely ungenteel. She then wished for a supper; he declared they must rise early. She accused him of making riches more melancholy than poverty; he answered that poverty, at least, had not obliged them to avoid Bow Street. This plain speech produced tears, reproaches, and finally one of those reconciliations in which neither party forgives any thing, but both are too tired to continue.

When he was asleep, or appeared to be so, Lydia sat before the glass in her travelling dress. It was new, becoming, and less showy than she liked; her bonnet had been chosen for quietness, a quality she still considered almost a personal insult. Yet the face beneath it was altered in a manner no milliner could remedy. It was not old; Lydia would have rejected the word with indignation. But something had passed over it—not sorrow, for sorrow refines only where there is something to refine; not penitence, for that was yet far distant; but a fretful fatigue, a discontent which had ceased to sparkle. She looked at herself earnestly, then smiled, to see whether the old expression would return. It did return, but not for long.

She opened the little enamel box again. The ribbon and paper lay within, foolishly preserved, useless, and therefore safe from Wickham’s cupidity. She thought of Elizabeth at her writing-table; of Jane’s miniature lying unremoved because it had not been worth taking; of Longbourn in the days when the greatest evil she knew was being kept from Meryton by rain; of Kitty following her, Mary moralising at her, her mother exclaiming, her father laughing, Elizabeth observing, Jane loving. These recollections came not tenderly, but with a strange irritation. They seemed to belong to a Lydia who had been noisy, foolish, happy, and not yet afraid.

“Well,” she whispered, shutting the box, “Lizzy had every thing after all. It was very easy for her to be good.”

This reflection comforted her, as many unjust reflections do, by restoring the speaker to injury. She put the box away carefully.

At dawn they left their lodging. London was then in that grey condition when even guilt looks sleepy and virtue has not yet put on its best appearance. The streets were damp; shutters closed; a few carts moved heavily; women swept doorsteps with an industry which poverty seldom permits to be ornamental. Lydia, chilled and cross, complained of the hour, the air, the carriage, and the necessity of being called Mrs. Vernon for the journey.

“I shall forget it,” said she. “I know I shall. I never could remember names when they were ugly.”

“Then remember that Mrs. Wickham is at present less safe.”

“I am sure Mrs. Wickham has had very little safety at any time,” she replied; and for once the observation was just.

As the coach carried them southward, London receded without ceremony, retaining no more impression of them than it retains of multitudes who come to be enriched, concealed, tempted, or undone. Wickham grew easier with every mile, though not cheerful; Lydia grew excited as soon as she could think of France without thinking of why they went there. She spoke of Paris again, of balls, of being taken for a foreign lady of distinction, of writing to Jane when all was settled—“only not telling her where we are till it is quite convenient”—and of the astonishment which must one day seize Elizabeth when she learned that her youngest sister had contrived to be elegant in spite of everybody.

Wickham listened, smiled when it was necessary, and looked often behind him.

It is a melancholy truth, though not one to be mentioned in mixed company without caution, that successful wrong commonly imagines its chief difficulty over at the very moment when punishment has only changed its road. The Wickhams had converted Pemberley into money, and money into the promise of flight. They had escaped servants, tradesmen, officers, and, as they believed, family suspicion. They had lost much of their plunder to the necessary rapacity of those who assisted them, but retained enough to set vanity dancing again. England was to be left; France was to receive them; and Lydia, whose spirits rose with the prospect of a new audience, began to think that Providence, after being long partial to her sisters, had at last remembered what was due to herself.

In this persuasion they proceeded to the coast: he with his pockets secured and his fears somewhat lulled, she with her bonnet adjusted, her conscience imperfectly awakened, and her imagination already furnished with rooms in which Elizabeth could not enter. The sea, of which Lydia thought only as a troublesome interval between English envy and French admiration, lay before them; and if it had any opinion of their plans, it expressed none.

## Chapter V  
### The Channel

The passage from Dover to the opposite coast has been performed by many persons of very different characters, and with very different degrees of self-approbation; by the hopeful bride, the disappointed politician, the idle youth in pursuit of novelty, the invalid in pursuit of climate, and the debtor in pursuit of that species of health which consists in being at a distance from his creditors. It was now to receive Mr. and Mrs. Wickham; and if the sea had ever been known to make distinctions in favour of beauty, address, or recent prosperity, Lydia might have embarked with more confidence than prudence had hitherto allowed her.

They reached Dover under names which did them no honour, but gave them at least the temporary advantage of not being their own. Mr. and Mrs. Vernon were entered upon such lists as the master of the packet thought proper to keep; and Lydia, who had at first declared that she should never remember to answer to so ugly an appellation, found, when a stranger looked at her with any appearance of curiosity, that fear is a very sufficient governess to the memory.

The day was unsettled. A pale sun had shown itself in the morning with that air of good intention which imposes upon sanguine travellers; but before noon the wind freshened, the clouds gathered with more haste than elegance, and the sea, which Lydia had fancied smooth because it was distant, assumed, on nearer acquaintance, a roughness very little calculated to recommend foreign travel. The packet lay uneasy at her moorings, rising and falling with a motion which made Mrs. Wickham exclaim against the cruelty of every body who had ever praised France.

“I am sure,” cried she, drawing her cloak about her with more petulance than warmth, “if Paris were not the most charming place in the world, nothing should induce me to go to it. The sea is the most odious contrivance! I wonder people submit to it.”

“People submit,” replied Wickham, who had been watching the quay with more attention than the horizon, “to many things when the alternative is worse.”

“That is always your way of talking now. You make every thing sound as if somebody were following us.”

“My dear Lydia, if nobody be following us, so much the better; but it would be imprudent to stay and ascertain the fact.”

She looked back towards the town. There were carts, sailors, a woman with baskets, two gentlemen in cloaks, and a boy running with a bundle. Any one of them, upon sufficient provocation from imagination, might have been an officer of justice; and Lydia, though inclined to despise caution whenever it interfered with amusement, had lately made discoveries in that science which rendered her less unwilling to profit by it.

Their luggage had been contrived with much art and some altercation. Wickham had reduced every thing which belonged to her comfort, and enlarged every thing which concerned his security. Lydia’s gowns, sacrificed to the necessities of secrecy, had been packed with a compression exceedingly injurious to their beauty; but the bank-notes, drafts, gold, and small jewels were disposed with a tenderness which proved that Mr. Wickham could be careful where affection was not required. Of these treasures he retained the principal command. Lydia, however, had not been idle. She had her little hoard, partly conceded, partly concealed; a few notes stitched where no laundress of ordinary curiosity would have looked without encouragement; two rings, whose want of setting made them less lovely but more portable; and the enamel box, which, being of no great pecuniary consequence, had escaped all contention.

That box, had any rational observer been admitted to Lydia’s reflections, would have furnished some surprise. She did not value it because it had belonged to Elizabeth; she would have resented such an interpretation. She kept it, as she told herself, because it was pretty, because it took no room, because Wickham would get nothing by it, and because she chose to keep it. These reasons were sufficient for Lydia; and if another, less distinct and less agreeable, lay beneath them, she had not yet been long enough under the influence of discomfort to examine it.

The vessel was not crowded with such company as she had once imagined proper to any conveyance which bore herself. There were two merchants, civil without gallantry; a French woman with a hood drawn over a face of much anxiety; a young man who was sick before they had lost sight of the harbour, and therefore ceased to be of the smallest consequence; a clergyman returning, as he said, from business connected with a living, though his countenance expressed no great satisfaction with either the business or the sea; and several persons whose appearance Lydia would formerly have dismissed as “very droll,” but whom she now considered chiefly as possible witnesses against her.

Wickham placed her where she was least observed, and then repented, because from that situation she could observe him. He had been accustomed, through life, to prefer his own arrangements uninspected. He moved among the few passengers with his usual readiness, said something easy to the merchants, something obliging to the French woman, and something facetious to the master of the vessel, who received it as men do who have too much wind to attend to wit. His spirits rose a little when the shore began to recede. England, seen from the deck, had already less authority than England seen from a lodging-house window; and danger, like a disagreeable relation, appeared more tolerable at every increase of distance.

Lydia, too, recovered sufficiently to be impatient.

“I cannot think,” said she, “why we could not have gone in a better vessel. If people have money, they ought at least to be comfortable.”

“Comfort is an excellent thing when it does not require one’s name,” answered Wickham.

“La! I am tired of hearing of names. Mrs. Vernon is gone to France, Mrs. Wickham is gone to France, what signifies which? I dare say, once we are in Paris, nobody will care. I shall say my husband is a gentleman of fortune.”

“You may say it with perfect truth,” replied he, smiling; “if fortune be allowed to include what a gentleman can secure.”

Lydia was pleased by this, though not quite understanding it as a reflection upon himself; and she began, while the vessel laboured onward, to furnish her future with the same liberality which had so often made amends for the poverty of her present. She saw herself in apartments of French elegance, among mirrors unspotted by former occupants and candles innumerable; she saw mantua-makers in a state of respectful agitation; she saw officers again, but foreign officers, with more lace and less knowledge of her history; she saw letters to Jane, so worded as to imply happiness without acknowledging obligation; and, most delightful of all, she saw Elizabeth hearing, through some channel not to be traced to herself, that Mrs. Wickham was admired in Paris.

Elizabeth had always been the great audience of Lydia’s imagination, though seldom the chosen confidante of her heart. To triumph before Jane would have brought softness with it, and softness was inconvenient; to triumph before her mother was too easy; to triumph before Kitty had lost its relish by being certain. But Elizabeth, who would neither envy loudly nor admire against judgment, supplied exactly that resistance which vanity loves to suppose itself conquering.

The wind increased. What had been rough became violent, and what had been merely disagreeable acquired the dignity of danger. The masters of small vessels are not apt to alarm their passengers by explanations; they prefer action, oaths, and silence, which, though perhaps necessary, are not consoling to ladies. The crew moved with haste; ropes were handled, orders called, a sail reduced; the vessel plunged and rose again in a manner which made all distinctions of rank, fortune, and intention exceedingly precarious. Lydia clung to the side of the little cabin, and declared, whenever speech was possible, that she had never been so ill-used in her life.

The French woman prayed softly. The clergyman, after an interval in which the colour of his face gave very little encouragement to his profession, attempted some words of composure, but they were lost in a crash above; and the young man who had been first sick now appeared to have advanced beyond all competition in misery. The merchants looked grave. Wickham, though pale, retained something of his manner, and went twice upon deck, partly to obtain intelligence, partly because in danger he had still the instinct of seeming useful when he could not be so.

On his second return, he found Lydia with her bonnet awry, her hair loosened, and her temper in that state when fear has not yet subdued vanity, but only made it more unreasonable.

“Tell them to go back!” she cried. “I will not be drowned in the middle of the water. I never agreed to any such thing. Tell the man we will pay him.”

“My dear,” said Wickham, with an attempt at lightness, “the sea is not commonly hired by the job.”

“I do not care. You said it would be quite safe.”

“I said we must go.”

“That is the same thing with you. You are always saying what is convenient and calling it true.”

The remark was not without merit; but the occasion did not allow it the applause to which, in a calmer hour, its justice might have entitled it. A tremendous lurch threw her against him; he caught her, less from tenderness than necessity, and for a moment they stood together, two beings united by law, danger, and mutual accusation, with very little else to recommend the connexion.

There are moments in which the most practised selfishness is forced into a semblance of feeling, as a disordered mirror may catch the light. Wickham looked at Lydia then—young still, terrified, foolish, pretty in distress in the way that prettiness may survive every claim on respect—and he perhaps remembered the laughing girl at Brighton who had flattered him by thinking ruin an adventure. If he did, the recollection was short; for another cry from above, sharper than the rest, restored him to the more useful consideration of his pockets.

The violence of the weather had by this time changed into something beyond the passengers’ power to describe, and perhaps beyond their captain’s power to approve. Water came where water ought not; motion became confusion; confusion, alarm. The sea, which in poetry is often employed to elevate the sentiments, had here the less refined effect of making every body cold, wet, and helpless. Lydia’s new travelling dress, chosen with so much dispute and intended to give a sober impression of consequence, clung to her with a humility exceedingly mortifying. Her bonnet, which had never pleased her, lost even the little merit of restraint; ribbons, feathers, and curls became one indiscriminate grievance.

It was then made known—not by formal announcement, for ceremony had been long abandoned—but by the faces and movements of those who understood such matters, that the packet was in conditions very unfavourable to the continuation of any passenger’s plans. A boat was spoken of; then not spoken of; a spar broke; something gave way; cries increased; a child, whom Lydia had scarcely noticed before, began to scream with a perseverance which, at another time, she would have called monstrous. The men urged the passengers to move; the passengers, not knowing where, impeded them by obeying in the wrong direction.

Wickham seized Lydia’s arm.

“Keep by me,” said he.

“I cannot! Where are we going? George, do not leave me!”

It was perhaps the first time for many months that she had used his Christian name without either reproach or coquetry. He heard it, or seemed to hear it; but at that instant a rush of water, a blow, a darkness of bodies and timber, divided them. Lydia was thrown against something hard, then down, then up again, with salt in her mouth and a noise in her ears which seemed to her, in that first astonishment, less like the sea than like all the people she had ever known speaking at once.

She called to him. Whether she called “Wickham” or “George,” she never afterwards knew; and as “afterwards” was granted her only in minutes, the uncertainty was of no consequence. She saw, through spray and dusk, a figure which might have been his, struggling towards the place where a boat had been lowered or overturned; she saw a hand raised—not in farewell, for Mr. Wickham had never been profuse in gestures where they could not advantage him—but in the mere contest for life; she heard a shout, broken off. Then he was gone from her sight.

It would be unjust to assert that, at that moment, Lydia thought chiefly of his crimes. She thought that she was alone. The distinction is important; for many wives of better principles might, under the same circumstances, have reached no higher reflection. She grasped whatever came within her hands, was struck, swallowed water, rose again, and found herself clinging to a piece of shattered timber, the vessel no longer a creature but a wreck, the sky low and black, the shore invisible, and France as unattainable as prudence had always been.

How long she remained thus, no clock, no companion, and no composure could have told. Time in such extremities loses its politeness, and will not advance by any habits familiar to drawing-rooms. There were cries at first—near, then farther, then fewer. A barrel floated by; a hat, absurdly genteel, turned over and disappeared; a length of rope, a fragment of painted wood, something white that might have been a shawl, were seen and lost. Lydia held fast. Her fingers, which had been employed so lately in examining lace and counting notes, acquired a tenacity for which no one would have given them credit.

The cold was very great. It entered not as an attack, but as a settlement, taking possession by degrees, and leaving less and less of her to dispute it. The packet of notes she had concealed was heavy with water against her; the rings, for which she had risked discovery in London, were still somewhere upon her person, and had never appeared more useless. The enamel box was gone. Whether it had fallen from her before the wreck, or slipped from its hiding-place in the struggle, she could not know. Its loss gave her a small, sharp pang, disproportionate to its worth, and therefore not wholly concerned with property.

She tried to be angry at the sea, and found it difficult. The sea had no countenance to resent, no pride to wound, no sister to rival, no husband to reproach. It neither admired nor accused her. It took no interest in whether she had been first married, handsomely dressed, neglected, envied, or ill-used. Such indifference was new to Lydia, and more awful than condemnation.

Her thoughts, when they settled at all, went first to trifles. She wondered whether her bonnet could be seen; whether her hair was quite spoiled; whether Wickham had the larger sum upon him, and whether it would sink faster in gold than in paper; whether Paris were very bright that night; whether Jane would hear of the wreck before Elizabeth; whether her mother would scream; whether her father would say something bitter and then be silent. These considerations passed through her mind without order, and with a clearness oddly unlike reflection. Fear had exhausted itself; pain was becoming less particular; and in the room left empty by terror, memory entered uninvited.

Longbourn came first—not as she had last seen it, with mortifications, reproofs, and the consciousness of being troublesome, but in some earlier condition of summer mornings, open doors, her mother’s voice, Kitty’s laughter following her own, Mary at the instrument, Jane’s fairness bent over work, Elizabeth walking in from the shrubbery with that look of amusement which Lydia had always suspected of meaning more than it said. She remembered the importance of ribbons, the triumph of officers in the neighbourhood, the misery of a rainy day, the glory of being talked of. How small the world had been then, and how completely she had contrived to fill it!

She did not accuse herself at once. Habit is a faithful servant, even to bad masters. She thought first that nobody had understood her. Jane had loved her, but had never known how lively spirits required indulgence. Elizabeth had seen too much and pardoned too little; or pardoned in a manner more provoking than severity. Darcy—Mr. Darcy—had always judged; though, to do him justice, he had not often said so. Her father had laughed when he ought to have guided; her mother had encouraged when she ought to have restrained. Wickham had flattered, and she had believed him. Every body, in their several ways, had failed her.

This arrangement of blame was comfortable, and she clung to it for several moments as strongly as to the timber. But comfort, under such circumstances, is a poor swimmer. Another thought rose after it, slowly and coldly, yet with a steadiness she could not dismiss. If they had all failed her, why had Jane not become Lydia? Jane, who had been admired without noise, beloved without pursuit, happy without display. If Elizabeth had been proud, why had pride in her produced care, while pride in Lydia had produced only defiance? If fortune had been partial, why had she herself, whenever kindness was offered, turned it into a jest, a claim, or an opportunity?

She did not put these questions into language. Lydia Wickham was not at once transformed, by danger and salt water, into a philosopher. But the sense of them passed over her; and for the first time in her life she was not quite able to laugh it away.

Jane’s face returned with particular distinctness—not shocked now, nor reproachful, but sorrowing in that tender, helpless way which had often vexed Lydia because it imposed gratitude without giving occasion for quarrel. Jane would cry when she knew. Jane would remember only what could soften the story; would say that her poor Lydia had been led wrong, had suffered much, had not known what she did. This anticipated forgiveness, which once would have pleased her as a tribute, now gave her a discomfort almost resembling shame.

“She was always too good,” Lydia thought; and even then there was a touch of petulance in it. “It was easy for her. Every body loved Jane.”

Then Elizabeth. Here feeling was less gentle. Lydia saw her at Pemberley, mistress of rooms where nothing was tawdry, wife of a man whose silence could protect more effectually than other men’s compliments, sister become superior without ceasing to be sister. She saw again the drawer opened by her own hand, the little objects taken because they were valuable, and others because they had lain unguarded; the neat paper, the ribbon, the miniature of Jane which she had left behind, not from delicacy, but because it could not readily be sold. She remembered thinking that Elizabeth had more than enough, that a little diminution of splendour would do her no harm, that a sister might surely take where a sister had been so provokingly fortunate.

The argument appeared to her now very poor. It did not cease to be tempting; she still felt, with a stubborn remnant of the old Lydia, that Elizabeth’s lot had been unreasonably full. Why should Lizzy, who had once been no more than the second Bennet girl, sit among plate and jewels, respect and consequence, while she herself was to be scolded, pitied, or forgotten? The injustice of the distribution had long been one of Lydia’s principal articles of faith. Yet beneath it, not destroying but chilling it, came another perception—that Elizabeth had not taken her happiness by clamour, nor kept it by falsehood; that esteem, dull as it had once appeared, might perhaps be a safer foundation for joy than admiration; that being envied was not the same as being loved; that a room full of candles cannot warm a mind which has made every kindness an affront.

This was a hard lesson, and Lydia had no time to learn it gracefully.

Her hold weakened; she shifted her arm and gasped as a wave passed over her. The heavens had darkened into one vast cloud, but low down, far off, there seemed once a light—perhaps from a vessel, perhaps from imagination, perhaps from that shore to which she had been carrying stolen hopes and a stolen fortune. She stared at it until it vanished. She wondered whether Wickham had reached any thing to support him. The thought of his death did not produce the tenderness which novels might require; it brought instead a tired anger, a trace of fear, the memory of his smile, and the knowledge that she had chosen him because he made folly look agreeable.

“He said I should have every thing,” she thought.

It was a childish reproach; but much of Lydia had remained childish, except where vice had made her old.

The cold had now so far prevailed that pain itself was less importunate. Her mind, released from the body’s complaints by the body’s failure to continue them, grew strangely quiet. She thought of the first ball at which an officer had asked her to dance; of Kitty’s envy; of her mother’s delight; of her own certainty that life could offer no higher distinction than to be looked at. She thought of Brighton, of the elopement, not as romance now, but as a noisy room from which all the candles had burned out. She thought of Darcy’s interference, of money paid, disgrace covered, marriage contrived; of all the labour which had been expended to give her a second chance, and how impatiently she had worn it, like a gown made by another person’s taste.

Had she gone home differently—had she listened, written, waited, owned herself foolish, endured one month of humiliation without seeking to turn it into triumph—what then? The picture would not form completely. A respectable life was too quiet for her imagination, even at the end. But she saw, in fragments, Jane visiting with children about her knees; Elizabeth speaking sharply perhaps, but smiling afterwards; Kitty less silly because she had not Lydia to follow; her father looking at her with less irony; her mother boasting of her without fear. She saw herself not splendid, not envied, not the first in any thing, but safe. The word had little brilliance, yet at that moment it seemed to contain all the comforts she had spent her life in despising.

“I might have been—” she began; but the sentence had no finish. She did not know what she might have been. Better, perhaps; happier, perhaps; less admired, certainly. The certainty still mortified her.

A wave turned the timber. She struggled with an instinct which had outlived hope. The notes within her dress tore loose and spread upon the water, pale scraps without value, fluttering for an instant before they disappeared. Lydia saw them go with a faint astonishment. So much contrivance, so much fear, so many lies, and the sea took them without even counting. The rings followed, or remained; it did not signify. Somewhere below, perhaps, Mr. Darcy’s silver, altered beyond recognition, had purchased gold which now purchased nothing; family jewels had become drafts, drafts had become soaked paper, and pride, passing through many hands, ended by being lighter than foam.

If Lydia could have smiled, the thought might almost have amused her. There was a justice in it too neat for her former taste.

Her last clear thought was not of Wickham. It was of her sisters standing together at Longbourn before any of them were married: Jane serene, Elizabeth bright-eyed and arch, herself laughing louder than either, determined that no pleasure should pass without her taking more than her share. She felt again, not affection exactly, but the ache of something wasted. Jane would have loved her still. Elizabeth, if severe, had not wished her lost. She had called their goodness dull because it had not clapped its hands for her; and she had mistaken every warning for envy, because envy was the only tribute she had understood.

“Lizzy had every thing,” she thought once more; but the old resentment was fainter now, worn thin by cold and truth. “Yet she would not have done this.”

It was as near to confession as Lydia Wickham ever came.

The timber slipped. Whether her fingers opened or the water forced them, no human witness was near enough to distinguish. The sea received her with the same want of ceremony with which it had received her fortune, her disguises, her complaints, and her hopes of Paris. There was no speech, no spectacle, no becoming arrangement of hair and drapery such as imagination might have chosen. A young woman who had once thought the eyes of a regiment necessary to her happiness passed out of the world unobserved.

Of Mr. Wickham, it was afterwards said that no certain account could be given. A body was found some days later at a distance down the coast, and another not found at all; a pocket-book washed ashore without its owner; a fragment of luggage was claimed by nobody; and the sea, which is the least communicative of witnesses, kept its own counsel. Those who had known Mr. Wickham were thus spared the trouble of deciding whether to pity, condemn, or merely remember him as they had last found him—a gentleman of pleasing address, excellent grievances, and no principle sufficiently heavy to sink before his money.

The news reached Derbyshire in due course, attended by the usual inaccuracies which render calamity more fit for conversation. At Pemberley there was grief, but not of the ostentatious kind. Elizabeth mourned less for the silver lost than for the sister who had made herself irrecoverable long before the sea completed the work. Jane wept as Lydia had foreseen, and forgave more than Lydia had deserved; which is perhaps one of the few extravagances of goodness that do no harm to society. Mr. Darcy, who had never expected gratitude from the Wickhams and had ceased to expect amendment, felt the solemn melancholy of seeing trust violated and life thrown away; but he spoke little, and acted where action remained possible.

The heirlooms were not restored. A few altered stones, a broken crest, and some pieces of plate too much disfigured for identification found their way, after many months, into hands which suspected without proving. But Pemberley, having endured worse profanation than loss, resumed its quiet. Drawers were locked with more care; servants were examined with more caution; visits of near relations were considered with more judgment. Such are the improvements by which the innocent commonly pay for the instruction of the guilty.

As for Lydia Wickham, there were those who remembered her as the lively Mrs. Wickham, those who remembered her as poor Lydia, and those who, with less charity but perhaps not less truth, remembered that liveliness without sense is a dangerous recommendation in a world very ready to applaud it for half an hour. She had desired notice above all things, and obtained at last a paragraph in a newspaper, where her assumed name deprived even misfortune of its proper distinction. The world went on; assemblies were crowded, officers were admired, young ladies laughed too loud, mothers encouraged too much, fathers laughed too easily, and sisters, wiser or kinder than they were valued for being, continued to offer counsel which the vain might call pride and the foolish restraint.

If there is a justice suited to such a history, it is not in noise or dreadful example, but in the quiet proportion by which empty wishes defeat themselves. Lydia had pursued admiration till she was beneath respect; had grasped at splendour till she lost safety; had envied happiness till she could no longer comprehend it; and had fled from disgrace only to find a place where disguise was of no use. The sea made no moral reflection upon her, and neither shall we overburden her end with one. It is enough to observe that, having spent her life in mistaking clamour for consequence, she passed from it at last into a silence which no vanity could disturb.
